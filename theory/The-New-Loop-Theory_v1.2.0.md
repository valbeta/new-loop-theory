# The New Loop Theory (降维创新理论) V1.2.0

## Abstract

The New Loop Theory is a theoretical research framework concerning the evolution of business systems. It addresses a long-standing question in innovation and strategy: why some emerging forces, originally positioned at the periphery, can ultimately alter the operating mechanisms of the entire competitive system, causing previously decisive advantages—such as enterprises, resources, and capabilities—to gradually lose their dominant role.

Unlike traditional competitive analysis, The New Loop Theory does not initially focus on direct comparisons between enterprises, nor does it attempt to answer "who can defeat whom." Instead, it addresses more fundamental questions: How is value generated, accumulated, and migrated within a business system? What determines long-term competitive outcomes—is it a specific advantage possessed by an enterprise, or the system structure capable of continuously generating advantages?

This theory views the business system as a Complex Adaptive System (CAS) and proposes the "Loop" as the fundamental unit of analysis for understanding business system evolution. From this perspective, an enterprise's competitive advantage is not statically embedded in a specific product, technology, resource, or brand. Rather, it is the continuous outcome of a series of feedback relationships under specific system conditions.

A mature business system is typically sustained by existing loops. Old loops form stable advantages through resource accumulation, capability precipitation, and organizational adaptation. However, new **value creation** mechanisms may emerge at the periphery of the competitive system, gradually forming a value accumulation logic distinct from the existing system. When a new loop establishes its own reinforcing mechanisms and continuously accumulates momentum, the flow of value within the competitive system may migrate, ultimately leading to changes in the competitive structure.

The New Loop Theory proposes three core conditions to describe the evolutionary capacity of a new loop: **Asymmetric** in the logic of value creation, **Amplifying** in the nature of internal dynamic mechanisms, and **Ascendant** in the direction of system evolution. These three collectively constitute the causal chain from the formation of a new loop to the change in system structure.

**The New Loop Theory** focuses not on how an enterprise defeats the **old loop**, but on how a **new loop** forms, causing the advantages upon which the old loop relies within the existing business system to gradually lose their dominant role.

The current version does not attempt to be a predictive model, nor does it provide specific innovation tools. It focuses on a more fundamental theoretical question:

> **Under what structural conditions can a new loop form within an existing business system and ultimately alter the direction of value accumulation?**

# 1. Problem Origins

## 1.1 Semantic Shift of "Disruptive Innovation" in the Chinese Context

In the development of modern innovation theory, "Disruptive Innovation" is undoubtedly one of the most influential concepts. However, during its dissemination in the Chinese context, the term "颠覆" (disruption/subversion) has gradually undergone a certain degree of semantic shift.

In the daily Chinese context, "颠覆" usually implies overthrowing, defeating, replacing, or even destroying. This linguistic reinforcement leads many innovation practitioners to form a relatively direct imagination of competition: innovation means finding a way to challenge leaders and ultimately defeat existing players.

This understanding tends to shape the innovation process into a direct confrontational relationship:

> Entrepreneurs challenging giants, latecomers defeating leaders, and new models replacing old ones.

If innovation is understood as "finding a way to defeat giants," attention naturally shifts to competitors: where to attack, how to surpass, and how to prove one's product is superior.

This understanding reinforces the confrontational narrative in innovation but weakens the more critical question in the original theory:

**Why do some innovations that initially appear marginal, weak, or even inconspicuous ultimately change the entire market structure?**

In fact, many new forces that alter the industrial landscape do not initiate direct frontal competition with the old loops within the existing business system. They often establish a different value creation mechanism outside the focus of the old loops, and gradually change the competitive environment as they grow.

Therefore, The New Loop Theory is neither a renaming of "Disruptive Innovation" nor an attempt to replace existing innovation theories. It attempts to address a more fundamental question: If innovation does not mean competitive confrontation, what is the true mechanism driving changes in system structure?

A further inquiry is: **What kind of new loop can form, accumulate, and change the system structure itself within an existing competitive system?**

## 1.2 From Competitive Outcomes to System Evolution

Traditional business analysis usually begins with comparisons between enterprises:  

> _Who has stronger technology?_  
> _Who has more users?_
> _Who has a larger market share?_
> _Who has higher efficiency?_

These questions focus on competitive outcomes, but the business system is not a static chessboard.

Every decision made by an enterprise alters resource allocation; resource allocation changes organizational capabilities; organizational capabilities affect products and services; products and services influence user behavior; user behavior further impacts revenue, data, brand, and ecosystem. These changes are interconnected and continuously feed back into the system itself.

Therefore, an enterprise's advantage today is likely not brought about by a single isolated factor.

What truly deserves attention is: **Why can these factors continuously reinforce each other and sustainably generate advantages?**

From this perspective, the users, brands, channels, technologies, and organizational capabilities possessed by an enterprise are merely stocks within the business system. The more critical question is: Through what loops are these stocks connected, and in what environment can these loops continuously generate returns?

The markets, customers, channels, supply chains, organizational capabilities, technological capabilities, and capital resources faced by a mature enterprise have formed mutually reinforcing relationships after long-term competition. These relationships are not just a set of advantages possessed by the enterprise; together, they constitute the enterprise's way of continuously creating value.

After a value accumulation mechanism has operated for a sufficiently long time, the resource allocation methods, organizational capabilities, and evaluation standards formed around it will increasingly adapt to this mechanism. Thus, a system structure that originally helped an enterprise succeed may gradually restrict the enterprise from discovering another value creation method.

This explains a long-standing phenomenon: successful enterprises are not necessarily the most adept at creating their own substitutes. The problem is not merely that the organization is "not innovative enough," but that the old loop itself is continuously reinforcing the competitive dimensions it is familiar with.

## 1.3 The Problems This Theory Attempts to Explain

The New Loop Theory attempts to explain a specific type of change: A new value creation method may initially be small or even outside the mainstream market. It does not immediately defeat the original enterprises, nor does it immediately capture the largest market share. However, it begins to establish its own feedback relationships.

As feedback continues to occur, the new loop gradually acquires more users, data, capabilities, resources, or ecosystem relationships. These newly added stocks further enhance the new loop, endowing it with an increasingly strong self-reinforcing capacity.

Meanwhile, system conditions are also changing. When these changes accumulate to a certain extent, the advantages that originally supported the old loop may no longer hold the same value. The old loop in the existing competitive system does not necessarily disappear suddenly, but the way it accumulates value begins to change.

The New Loop Theory focuses on this process, along with related questions such as:

> _Why do some innovations that initially appear merely as marginal changes gradually form their own growth mechanisms?_
> _Why does having users for a new product not necessarily mean it has established a new competitive logic?_
> _Why might the advantages accumulated by an old loop suddenly lose their original value after system conditions change?_

And the more critical question:

> _**Under what conditions can a new local loop develop into the new ascendant loop of the system?**_

These questions and changes collectively constitute the research object of The New Loop Theory.

# 2. Core Propositions

This theory first observes the structural evolution between old loops and new loops at the competitive system level, and further traces the key structures of value accumulation within the loops.

## 2.1 The Business System as a Complex Adaptive System

The business system is not a simple combination of static variables. Enterprises, users, suppliers, channels, technologies, capital, and institutions interact continuously, and every action alters the future behavioral conditions of other participants.

Therefore, the outcome of a business system is often not determined directly by a single factor, but is the result of the long-term interaction of multiple feedback relationships.

From the perspective of a complex adaptive system, competitive advantage is no longer just an attribute "possessed" by an enterprise. It is more like a result continuously produced by the system under specific conditions.

Enterprises possess technological capabilities, brands, users, channels, or data, all of which belong to stocks; but how much value these stocks can generate depends on the system conditions they are in and the loops through which they are connected.

## 2.2 Old Loops and New Loops

An old loop is the value creation and accumulation mechanism that currently occupies a dominant position in the competitive system. It is usually composed of a series of mutually reinforcing causal relationships and forms a stable resource allocation method through long-term competition.

A new loop, on the other hand, is a value creation and accumulation mechanism that is structurally different from the old loop. It can start from the periphery of the existing competitive system or from a neglected condition within the existing competitive system itself.

A new loop does not initially need to be "larger" than the old loop. What truly matters is that it begins to generate value according to different causal relationships and forms its own accumulation mechanism within these different causal relationships.

Therefore, the comparison between new and old loops should not first fall on product functions or single advantages, but on the level of **how value is created, how feedback is formed, and how it is continuously accumulated**.

## 2.3 The Meaning of System Substitution

The "substitution" mentioned in The New Loop Theory does not mean that old enterprises, old products, or old capabilities must disappear.

An old loop can continue to exist and even retain commercial value for a long time. What truly changes is the way this old loop generates value within the system.

When a new loop continuously changes system conditions, the original stocks of the old loop may still exist, but the value they create is different from the past. Thus, the old loop loses its original reinforcing capacity, while the new loop begins to become a more stable path for value accumulation.

Therefore, substitution primarily manifests as a **change in the dominant path of value accumulation**, rather than a simple change in market share.

## 2.4 Core Definition

The New Loop Theory studies: How new value creation and accumulation loops form within existing business systems, and through self-reinforcing mechanisms, stock accumulation, and changes in system conditions, ultimately may become the new dominant value accumulation path of the competitive system.

# 3. Naming the Theory

The Chinese name of the theory is “降维创新.” The English name is The New Loop Theory. These are not literal translations of one another. Each name reflects a different aspect of the same theoretical proposition and is deliberately retained in its respective language.

In it's Chinese name, **"降维 (dimensionality reduction)"** refers to a shift in the level at which an innovation problem is observed. Rather than remaining within the visible dimensions of competition, the analysis moves toward the underlying structures and value relationships that generate those dimensions.

Accordingly, "dimensionality reduction" corresponds to a specific path of observation:

> **Events → Patterns → Structures → Mental Models**

One descends from events to structures, and from structures one seeks new possibilities. Truly significant opportunities for innovation sometimes lie not in the dimension over which current competitors are contending, but in the deeper structures from which that dimension of competition itself emerges.

**The New Loop Theory** takes its English name from the central object of inquiry: the emergence and evolution of new value-accumulation loops within existing competitive systems. “New” does not imply that every element of a New Loop must itself be new. Existing stocks, technologies, capabilities, or resources may be reorganized within a different value relationship and thereby participate in a different accumulation mechanism.

The two names therefore emphasize different dimensions of the theory. “降维创新” emphasizes the shift in perspective required to recognize a different underlying structure; The New Loop Theory emphasizes the new value-accumulation mechanism that may emerge from that shift. Neither name is intended as a literal translation of the other.

This deliberate difference in naming also reflects a concern informed by the history of “Disruptive Innovation.” When a theoretical concept enters a different linguistic and cultural context, its translated name can acquire semantic associations that differ from the concept's original theoretical meaning. Over time, those associations may influence how the concept is understood, applied, and further developed. The distinction between “降维创新” and The New Loop Theory is therefore intentional: it is designed to reduce the risk that the theory's name itself becomes a source of semantic drift.

# 4. Loop: The Basic Unit of Theoretical Analysis

## 4.1 What is a Loop

In this theory, a loop is not a rhetorical device to describe "circulation."

It refers to a set of causal relationships in which variables influence one another and form feedback loops. When a certain outcome can in turn affect the conditions that produced it, further enhancing or inhibiting the original change, the system has formed a feedback.

Therefore, the key to a loop is not "having many factors," but the existence of a continuous causal closed-loop among these factors.

From this perspective, an enterprise's competitive advantage can be re-understood: advantage is not just the resources an enterprise possesses, but the loop formed among certain resources, behaviors, and outcomes that can continuously reinforce value creation.

It should be noted that a loop does not imply that all feedback relationships within it have the same level of importance. For a specific new loop, it is necessary to further identify which feedback relationships bear the primary role in value accumulation, and which stocks constitute the key carriers of this accumulation.

## 4.2 Reinforcing Loop

Reinforcing loops cause changes in the system to continue expanding in the same direction.

For example, more users bring more feedback, feedback helps improve the product, product improvement enhances user value, thereby attracting more users. What truly matters here is not "user growth," but the mutually reinforcing causal relationship between user growth and product improvement.

Therefore, growth itself cannot prove the existence of a reinforcing loop.

An enterprise can achieve rapid growth through capital investment, price subsidies, or external resources, but if this growth does not form an internal feedback structure that can in turn enhance itself, then this growth does not equal **self-amplification**.

## 4.3 Balancing Loop

Balancing loops counteract changes in the system, driving it towards a certain stable state.

A large number of processes, rules, risk controls, resource allocations, and evaluation mechanisms in mature organizations may serve this function. They help the organization maintain stability and help the system maintain stability over time.

However, balancing loops are not the only source of organizational rigidity. Resource specialization, path dependence, existing interest relationships, organizational structures, and evaluation systems can all further reinforce the old loops.

Therefore, understanding the stability of old loops requires placing these factors back into the overall system for observation.

## 4.4 Stock

Stocks are the states or resources accumulated by the system through past activities, which can influence future system behavior.

Users, data, brands, technological capabilities, channels, organizational capabilities, ecosystem partners, and capital resources can all become stocks.

Stocks themselves are not synonymous with competitive advantage. How much value a stock can truly generate needs to be reflected through specific loops.

This is also a key distinction in The New Loop Theory: A new loop does not necessarily require the complete elimination of the stocks already possessed by the old loop. What it may change are the system conditions in which these stocks are situated, and the way these stocks generate value.

## 4.5 Gain Function

The same stock may generate completely different value under different system conditions.

Therefore, the competitive or value returns that a certain stock can generate under specific system conditions can be understood as its gain function.

It can be simply expressed as:

> **Gain_old(S, C₀) ≠ Gain_old(S, C₁)**

Where S represents the existing stock, and C represents system conditions.

This means that a resource originally very powerful for an enterprise does not naturally guarantee it will have the same value in the future. One of the important roles of a new loop is precisely to change system conditions, thereby altering the gain functions of the stocks that give the old loop its advantages.

This is a key entry point for understanding "why old advantages become invalid."

## 4.6 Attractor

Complex systems do not develop in all directions limitlessly. After long-term feedback, the system tends to move towards certain relatively stable states or paths, which can be understood using the concept of attractors.

In a business system, an attractor can be understood as the dominant value accumulation path that the system occupies over the long term.

Therefore, the true **Ascendant** state of a new loop is not simply "becoming number one in the market," but rather it beginning to become the system's more primary and stable value accumulation path.

This is why the ultimate change The New Loop Theory focuses on is not the sales volume of a certain product, but where the system itself is converging.

# 5. From Comparative Advantage to Comparative Loops

## 5.1 How Advantages are Continuously Produced by the System

Traditional competitive analysis easily understands advantage as a certain attribute possessed by an enterprise, such as cost, brand, technology, channel, or scale.

But if observed from a system perspective, these advantages are usually not statically existent.

Scale can bring cost advantages, cost advantages bring more customers, and customers further bring greater scale; users can generate data, data helps improve products, and improvements attract more users.

> Scale ↑ → Cost ↓ → Price ↓ → Customers ↑ → Scale ↑  
> Users ↑ → Data ↑ → Product Features ↑ → Users ↑

Advantages are therefore often the result of the continuous operation of a certain reinforcing loop.

## 5.2 Loop vs. Loop

If competition is merely comparing resource between enterprises, the core issue of innovation becomes "who has more."

But if competition occurs between loops, the problem changes.

A participant with fewer resources, as long as they can establish a different value creation loop, can avoid directly entering the original competitive dimensions. The early scale of a new loop can even be very small, because what it truly needs to establish is its own feedback structure.

Thus, innovation is no longer just about comparing who has stronger old advantages, but begins to compare: **Which loop can form stronger value accumulation in the future?**

## 5.3 System Dependence of Advantages

The value of an advantage depends on the system it is in.

A stock that is extremely important in the existing competitive system does not mean it will have the same gain under new system conditions. Conversely, a resource previously considered unimportant may suddenly have high value under new system conditions.

Therefore, a deep opportunity for innovation comes from redefining "what is valuable" and "why it is valuable."

This is also the fundamental reason why a new loop can change the competitive landscape.

# 6. When Old Loops Become Innovation Constraints

## 6.1 Loop Stabilization

After a loop has operated for a long time, it will produce more and more resources and capabilities that match itself.

The organization begins to recruit, evaluate, invest, and allocate resources according to this loop; customers and channels also gradually adapt to this transaction method.

Thus, the old loop is no longer just a value creation mechanism; it begins to shape the entire organization and market.

## 6.2 Rigidity Effects of Balancing Loops

At this point, the system will tend towards stability under the action of balancing loops, and stable systems need to suppress deviations.

New ideas usually imply uncertainty, resource reallocation, and a decline in short-term metrics; therefore, mature systems will naturally produce a large number of balancing feedback mechanisms.

These mechanisms themselves are not wrong. They are necessary for the organization to maintain stability.

The problem is that when the system needs to explore another loop, the mechanisms originally used to maintain stability may begin to inhibit the formation of the new loop.

## 6.3 Why Successful Systems Struggle to Create Their Own Substitutes

What a successful business system is best at is usually continuing to run the loops it has already validated.

It possesses capabilities, resources, processes, and evaluation standards highly matched with the old loops; therefore, any attempt to deviate from the old loops will face higher internal explanation costs.

This means that success itself may produce a structural constraint: the better the enterprise is at optimizing the current loops, the harder it is to establish a new loop outside the current loops.

This does not mean that mature enterprises cannot innovate, but it illustrates that another loop often needs to gain growth space at the periphery of the existing system or under a different logic of resource allocation.

# 7. The 3A Constraint Framework

The New Loop Theory uses three conditions to describe the basic constraints a new loop faces from its formation to becoming a system force: **Asymmetric, Amplifying, Ascendant**.

They are not three independent labels, but describe three levels of a new loop's evolution from structural formation, dynamic amplification, to system evolution:

- **Asymmetric**: Is it truly a different loop?
- **Amplifying**: Does it have the structural potential to make itself increasingly stronger?
- **Ascendant**: Has this force begun to change the system's dominant value accumulation path?

Therefore, the 3A framework corresponds to theoretical problems at three different levels: **Structure → Dynamics → System Evolution**.

The 3A framework is not a scoring model for predicting success, but a necessary condition framework for identifying whether a new loop possesses the potential for system evolution.

## 7.1 Asymmetric

**Asymmetric** refers to the structural differences between the new loop and the old loop in value creation, revenue formation, or competitive gain functions.

This difference cannot merely come from more functions, lower prices, or better experiences. A product can be excellent yet still operate within the old loop.

True asymmetry needs to answer:

> **If the new loop holds, does it create, obtain, and accumulate value in a different way from the old loop?**

Therefore, being asymmetric is a structural requirement for "newness."

### 7.1.1 Asymmetry is Not Just About Creating New Demand-Side Value

In the process of new loop formation, being asymmetric means the new loop must differ from the old loop in its value creation logic. However, case analysis shows that merely changing demand-side value is insufficient to form a truly new loop.

The business system includes both the demand side and the supply side. The demand side determines how user value is selected, obtained, and evaluated; the supply side determines whether the organization can continuously create, deliver, and accumulate this value in its own way.

If a new loop only changes the demand side, while the supply side still relies on the most expensive and scarce resources in the existing competitive system, it is actually still constrained by the cost structure of the old loop.

Therefore, true asymmetry capable of forming a new loop must ultimately extend to the value relationship between demand and supply.

### 7.1.2 Bilateral Asymmetric Consistency

The structural asymmetry of a new loop does not require simultaneous changes on both the demand and supply sides, but requires the formation of a value coupling relationship between the two that is different from the old loop.

Demand-side Asymmetric answers: **Why do users begin to choose a way of obtaining value that did not exist or was not viable in the past?**

Supply-side Asymmetric answers: **Why can the organization create, deliver, or accumulate this value in a way different from the old loop?**

Together, they determine whether the new loop has truly broken away from the old loop.

At this point, bilateral asymmetric consistency truly answers: **Has a new, mutually supportive relationship been formed between the new demand selection logic and the new supply creation logic?**

If it is only Asymmetric on the demand-side, users gain a new experience, but the enterprise still relies on the old loop's production methods. Such innovation may gain short-term market attention but is easily constrained by the old loop's cost structure.

If it is only Asymmetric on the supply-side, the enterprise possesses a new value creation method, but users cannot perceive the new value. Such innovation also struggles to form an effective market loop.

Therefore, a new loop does not require simultaneous changes in both directions, but ultimately needs to form differences on both the demand and supply sides that can support each other. Only when this new way of obtaining value can form a continuous value exchange and feedback with the supply side can it further enter a new value accumulation loop.

### 7.1.3 Case Observation: Quibi
> _**Nature of Case Observation:** This case is presented solely as an illustrative observation of the underlying theoretical mechanisms, intended to concretize the abstract concepts within the New Loop Theory. The case details are drawn from publicly available business histories and industry consensus, and should not be construed as rigorous business historiography or empirical academic research._

Quibi is a typical case of unilateral asymmetry.

It observed the trend of mobile video consumption and proposed short-form content, mobile viewing, and high-quality production.

From the demand side, Quibi indeed attempted to create a new viewing experience; but on the supply side, it still relied on the high-cost production, professional production teams, and copyright investments of the traditional film and television industry.

Its value creation method remained close to:

**Content Production → User Subscription → Revenue Covering Production Costs**

Therefore, Quibi changed the demand-side viewing scenario, but did not further form a new supply-demand value coupling relationship.

It needed to support a new consumption experience with the high-cost structure of the old loop, so its new value proposition did not fully translate into an independent value accumulation loop.

### 7.1.4 Theoretical Significance of Bilateral Asymmetric Consistency

Bilateral asymmetric consistency does not require all innovations to simultaneously change all links in the industry chain. What it focuses on is whether the new loop has changed the key value relationships affecting its long-term value accumulation.

An innovation can completely start from local changes on the demand side or the supply side. What is truly important is whether this local change can further form a new value exchange relationship with the other side, and ultimately establish its own feedback structure. If the new demand value must still rely on the supply structure of the old loop to be valid, then this change is difficult to break free from the cost and resource constraints of the old loop; conversely, if the new supply capability cannot form a value that users can perceive and choose, it cannot enter effective market feedback either.

Therefore, bilateral asymmetric consistency focuses not on the quantity of changes, but on whether a new coupling relationship is formed between the changes. Demand-side changes create new value choices, supply-side changes create new value realization methods, and the new coupling between the two makes it possible for local innovation to form a new value accumulation loop.

## 7.2 Amplifying

**Amplifying** refers to the existence of a structural self-reinforcing mechanism within the new loop, enabling the operation of the loop to in turn reinforce itself and drive the continuous accumulation of the key stocks it relies on.

Here, it is necessary to particularly distinguish between **Amplifying** nature and **growth**. Growth describes the result, while Amplifying describes the causal structure that produces this result. An existing loop can achieve rapid growth through capital investment, policy support, manual expansion, or market dividends, without thereby possessing self-amplifying capabilities; conversely, a new loop may grow slowly in its early stages, but as long as it has formed a feedback structure within itself that can continuously reinforce itself, it may possess strong self-amplifying potential.

Therefore, the question Amplifying truly needs to answer is not how fast a new loop is growing now, but: **Does this loop have the structural potential to make itself increasingly stronger?**

### 7.2.1 Reinforcing Loops and Self-Amplification

Growth phenomena are easily explained as reinforcing loops. Increases in users, transactions, data, or resources may all present some positive relationship, but these phenomena themselves cannot prove that the new loop has formed a self-amplifying structure.

Growth in a business system may come from continuous external capital input, or from subsidies, manual resource expansion, technological dividends, market cycles, or other one-way driving factors. These factors can all manufacture growth, but they do not necessarily form a feedback loop capable of reinforcing itself.

True self-amplification requires the existence of an endogenous feedback: the results produced by the operation of the loop can in turn reinforce other conditions within the loop, and further improve the loop's ability to continue operating and accumulating. That is to say, the loop does not simply "grow more because it has more resources," but the results produced by its own operation become the conditions for the next round of operation.

This distinction is particularly important for The New Loop Theory. Because if a new loop can only maintain growth by continuously obtaining external resources, then it may possess growth capability, but it has not yet proven that it possesses the structural power to change the competitive system.

### 7.2.2 Net Amplifying

A new loop not only needs to have a reinforcing loop, but also needs to possess structural amplifying potential sufficient to resist internal resistance within the system. The term "net" here does not refer to a value that can already be precisely calculated, but refers to the structural relationship formed between the amplifying mechanism within the new loop and the forces within the system that inhibit its accumulation.

During its operation, a new loop will inevitably be limited by resource consumption, organizational complexity, competitive reactions, user friction, and other balancing mechanisms. At the same time, certain results within it may continuously reinforce key stocks such as users, data, ecosystems, cognition, or others. Net amplifying focuses precisely on this power relationship: whether the amplifying potential generated by the new loop is sufficient to enable it to continuously reinforce itself while being subjected to system resistance.

Therefore, "*Net Amplifying ≈ Amplifying Potential - System Resistance*" can be used as an intuitive expression to understand net amplifying, but this expression should not currently be understood as a mathematically defined calculation formula. What it intends to express is the theoretical meaning of "net," rather than requiring us to be able to assign precise numerical values to amplifying potential and system resistance right now.

What net amplifying truly cares about is a structural question: 

> _As the new loop continues to operate, does it become increasingly conditioned to reinforce itself and make key stocks easier to continue accumulating?_

If a loop's growth must continuously rely on external input to be maintained, then this growth cannot prove it has self-amplifying capabilities; conversely, even if a new loop does not grow significantly in its early stages, as long as it has formed a structure within itself that can continuously reinforce key stocks, it may possess powerful net amplifying potential.

Therefore, net amplifying is closer to a **structural potential attribute**, rather than a growth indicator. It describes whether the new loop has internally formed a power structure sufficient to support its own evolution.

### 7.2.3 Reinforcing Loops and Balancing Loops

Reinforcing loops in complex business systems do not exist in isolation. After any new loop begins to operate, it will simultaneously be affected by various limiting factors within the system, and these limiting factors may in turn form balancing loops.

For example, an increase in the number of users may enhance network value and further attract more users to join, thereby forming a reinforcing loop.

> Users increase → Network value increases → More users join

But at the same time, the expansion of user scale may also bring increased service complexity, increased infrastructure pressure, and higher operating costs, thereby forming a balancing loop that inhibits growth.

> Users increase → Service complexity increases → Service costs increase → User experience declines

Therefore, whether a new loop has net amplifying cannot be determined by merely observing whether it has positive feedback, but must simultaneously observe the structural relationship between positive feedback and system balancing forces. What is truly important is not "whether there is a reinforcing loop," but whether the reinforcing loop has sufficient structural power to enable the key stocks relied upon by the new loop to continue accumulating in the presence of these balancing forces.

This is also the most important distinction between net amplifying and ordinary growth. Growth only indicates that stocks are changing, while net amplifying attempts to explain why this change can gradually reinforce the loop that produced it.

### 7.2.4 Case Observation: Bike-Sharing

Bike-sharing presented an obvious reinforcing loop in its early stages. After the increase in vehicle deployment, the coverage area expanded, users gained more usage opportunities, increased orders brought more operational data, and further supported dispatch optimization. This process was able to form obvious scale growth in the early stages.

> More vehicle deployment → Expanded coverage area → Increased user orders → Data accumulation → Dispatch optimization

But at the same time, there were also important balancing forces within the system. The expansion of vehicle scale meant increased vehicle wear and tear, rising maintenance and dispatch costs, and increased offline operational complexity. A larger scale did not mean that each additional vehicle could increase value in the same way; on the contrary, as the system scale expanded, the costs required to maintain this scale might also continuously increase.

> Vehicle scale expands → Vehicle wear and tear increases → Maintenance costs increase → Operational pressure rises  
> Vehicle scale expands → Dispatch complexity increases → Offline operational costs increase

Therefore, what the bike-sharing case illustrates is not that "growth did not occur," but that growth itself is insufficient to prove that a new loop has sufficient net amplifying potential. Only when the positive feedback brought by scale growth can be continuously transformed into key stock accumulation sufficient to offset system resistance can scale truly become the reinforcing mechanism of the new loop itself.

### 7.2.5 Net Amplifying and External Resources

External resources do not naturally mean that a loop lacks self-amplification. External resources such as capital, policies, infrastructure, and market education may all help a new loop cross the early formation stage. The key issue is not whether resources come from inside or outside the system, but whether these resources, after entering, become part of the new loop's own feedback structure.

If external resources can help the new loop establish key stocks such as users, data, technological capabilities, ecosystems, or others, and these stocks can in turn reinforce the new loop, then external resources can become trigger conditions or accelerating factors in the formation process of the new loop.

But if, after the cessation of continuous external input, key stocks immediately stop accumulating, it indicates that the system's growth mainly relies on external driving forces, rather than coming from the loop's own reinforcing mechanism. External resources can trigger, accelerate, or even amplify a new loop, but they cannot replace the new loop's own reinforcing structure.

Therefore, when analyzing a new loop, what truly needs to be judged is not "whether it used external resources," but: **After external resources stop continuous input, does the new loop still possess the structural potential to reinforce itself?**

### 7.2.6 Self-Amplification and Key Stocks

Self-amplification must ultimately be implemented in some key stock that can be continuously accumulated. Even if a loop continuously produces short-term results, if these results are not transformed into stocks that can in turn reinforce the loop, it is difficult to form a truly system force.

The stocks accumulated by different new loops may be completely different. It can be a user network, data, technological capabilities, developer ecosystem, brand trust, organizational capabilities, channel network, or even some kind of knowledge or cognitive asset. For a theory, a key stock can even manifest as mental model share.

Therefore, when analyzing self-amplification, it is necessary to further ask: **What exactly is this new loop accumulating? Will the increase of this stock, in turn, make it easier for the new loop to continue accumulating?**

If the answer is yes, then the reinforcing loop is no longer just a tool for describing growth phenomena, but begins to become a mechanism for explaining how a new loop gains system power.

## 7.3 Ascendant

**Ascendant** describes an evolutionary state that a new loop enters at the system level. When a new loop, through continuous development, begins to change system conditions and gradually becomes the system's more primary value accumulation path, it can be said to have entered an Ascendant state.

Therefore, being ascendant is not simply "winning market competition," nor is it a static market share indicator. It describes which value accumulation path in the system is becoming the main direction, and whether resources, users, and other key stocks are beginning to reorganize around this new path.

### 7.3.1 Ascendancy is Not a Static Result

A new loop becoming dominant does not mean it gains a permanent advantage. It means that under specific system states, this loop has become the main direction of value accumulation.

As new loops appear, the system may change again. Therefore, ascendancy needs to be understood as a dynamic state, rather than a final result. A loop can become the system's primary attractor in a certain stage, and subsequently lose this status due to changes in system conditions.

This means that ascendancy needs to be understood on two scales simultaneously. One scale focuses on whether the new loop has become the dominant value accumulation path in the current stage; the other scale focuses on whether this dominant path itself will migrate with system evolution. The former belongs to Ascendant, while the latter enters the long-term evolution process described by Sequential Ascendance.

### 7.3.2 Sequential Ascendance

Sequential ascendance does not negate ascendancy, but places ascendancy back into the complex business system for understanding. Dominance is not the endpoint, but the starting point for the next round of system evolution.

The business system is not a static environment. The formation of every new loop will change the behavior of participants, the direction of resource flow, and the path of value accumulation, and these changes will create new opportunity spaces, enabling new loops to form around the changed system conditions.

Therefore, **Sequential Ascendance** describes: **In a complex business system, the dominant value accumulation path continuously migrates with changes in system conditions. This migration may be realized through competition, substitution, or reconstruction between the new value loop and the original dominant loop.**

It needs to be specially noted here that Sequential Ascendance does not describe the continuous victories and defeats between enterprises or products, but the continuous migration of the system's dominant value accumulation path. A new dominant loop does not necessarily need to simply defeat the old loop, but may also gradually cause the original value accumulation path to lose its dominant position by recombining resources, changing value relationships, or reconstructing competitive conditions.

### 7.3.3 From Substitution Relationship to Attractor Competition

Traditional innovation narratives often adopt a linear substitution model: old technology has problems, new technology appears, and new technology ultimately replaces old technology. This model is applicable to some technology substitution scenarios, but is insufficient to explain long-term evolution in complex business systems.

In complex systems, a new loop does not just defeat the old loop, but changes what resources are worth accumulating and which feedback relationships can continuously generate value. The old loop can continue to exist, but gradually loses the concentration of system resources and user attention; the new loop changes the resource flow in the system through a new value accumulation method.

Therefore, the evolution of the business system is closer to a continuous process of the old loop forming dominance, the new loop appearing, competition between different value accumulation methods, changes in system conditions, and the new loop becoming the new attractor. This process does not necessarily end with the disappearance of the old loop.

> Old loop forms dominance → New loop appears → Competition between different value accumulation methods → System conditions change → New loop becomes the new attractor

From this perspective, innovation competition is not a one-time victory or defeat, but a continuous evolution of different value accumulation loops revolving around the system attractor.

### 7.3.4 Sequential Ascendance and Changes in System Conditions

Why a once successful new loop will eventually lose its dominance is usually not because it suddenly loses value, but because system conditions have changed, making its original gain function no longer have the same effect.

The gain function of a new loop depends on its relationship with the system conditions at that time. When key conditions such as user behavior, technological foundation, cost structure, and industrial connection methods change, the advantages in the original loop may decrease, or even transform into new limitations.

Therefore, sequential ascendance is not simply latecomers defeating pioneers, but new system conditions creating new directions for value accumulation. What truly migrates is what is worth being continuously accumulated in the system, and what feedback relationships can continue to form value.

### 7.3.5 Case Observation: BlackBerry
> _**Nature of Case Observation:** This case is presented solely as an illustrative observation of the underlying theoretical mechanisms, intended to concretize the abstract concepts within the New Loop Theory. The case details are drawn from publicly available business histories and industry consensus, and should not be construed as rigorous business historiography or empirical academic research._

The development process of BlackBerry embodies a typical form of sequential ascendance. In the early stage of the smartphone industry, traditional mobile phone manufacturers mainly established value cycles around communication functions and hardware sales. BlackBerry, however, constructed a different value accumulation logic: enterprise mobile needs brought demand for secure communication capabilities, secure communication capabilities promoted enterprise deployment, enterprise deployment brought more enterprise users, and further reinforced the enterprise communication ecosystem.

**Enterprise mobile needs → Secure communication capabilities → Enterprise deployment → More enterprise users → Stronger enterprise communication ecosystem**

BlackBerry's core competitiveness was not merely mobile phone hardware, but enterprise mobile communication infrastructure. Under the system conditions at that time, this loop formed an obvious attraction. Enterprise customers gained security and efficiency improvements, organizational IT departments formed procurement needs, and more enterprise applications developed around this system. Therefore, BlackBerry once established a value loop with dominance.

Subsequently, the development of the mobile Internet changed the direction of system competition. Smartphones gradually transformed from "enterprise communication devices" to "general-purpose computing platforms," and the new value accumulation direction began to revolve around application ecosystems, developer participation, and user growth. The new competitive loop changed not just the functions of mobile phones, but redefined what resources were worth accumulating in mobile devices.

**Application ecosystem → Developer participation → User growth → More applications**

**The enterprise mobile communication loop once occupied dominance → System conditions changed → The general-purpose mobile computing ecosystem became the new dominant value accumulation direction.**

Under the new system conditions, the importance of BlackBerry's original advantages declined, and its closed secure system and dedicated ecosystem no longer possessed the same gain capacity. Therefore, BlackBerry's decline cannot be simply explained as "innovation failure," but can be observed as a migration of the dominant value accumulation path: the enterprise mobile communication loop once occupied dominance, and subsequently, the general-purpose mobile computing ecosystem became the new dominant value accumulation direction.

This is not an attempt to use this case to prove a specific causal chain, but to use this case to observe how the dominant value accumulation path migrates with changes in system conditions.

### 7.3.6 Case Observation: Skype
> _**Nature of Case Observation:** This case is presented solely as an illustrative observation of the underlying theoretical mechanisms, intended to concretize the abstract concepts within the New Loop Theory. The case details are drawn from publicly available business histories and industry consensus, and should not be construed as rigorous business historiography or empirical academic research._

Skype also demonstrates the mechanism of sequential ascendance. In the early days of Internet communication, traditional international communication relied on operator infrastructure and generated high costs through distance. Skype established a new value cycle through Internet technology: more users brought a richer communication network, and a richer network further increased communication value, thereby further attracting more users to join.

**More users → Richer communication network → Increased communication value → More users joining**

This loop changed the way communication value was formed, transforming cross-regional communication from a high-cost service to an Internet infrastructure capability. What Skype changed was not just the price of communication, but the fundamental way communication value was created and accumulated.

But as time went on, communication needs and system conditions continued to change. Users and enterprises no longer just needed "low-cost communication," but increasingly needed stable video conferencing experiences, enterprise collaboration capabilities, multi-person interaction, and organizational management capabilities. Subsequently, some products began to redefine value accumulation around the new demand structure, such as focusing on enterprise collaboration needs, stable meeting experiences, enterprise adoption, and enhanced product capabilities formed by validation in more organizational scenarios.

**Enterprise collaboration needs → Stable meeting experience → Enterprise adoption → More organizational scenario validation → Enhanced product capabilities**

Skype once changed the Internet communication system, but the dominant value accumulation path of Internet communication continued to migrate. The new loop redefined what communication value should be accumulated around, which is precisely the system evolution described by sequential ascendance.

## 7.4 Causal Chain

The three conditions of 3A can be understood within a basic dynamic relationship. Being **Asymmetric** first changes the structure of the new loop, making it no longer just a local optimization of the old loop; being **Amplifying** further determines whether this structural difference can be continuously reinforced through endogenous feedback and drive the accumulation of key stocks; when key stocks reach a certain level, the new loop may begin to influence system conditions and further change the dominant value accumulation path in the system, becoming **Ascendant**.

Therefore, the dynamic mechanism of 3A in a new loop can be expressed as:

**Emergence at the Edge → Asymmetric → Amplifying → Key Stock Accumulation → May begin to influence system conditions → Attractor Migration → Ascendant**

The arrows here describe a theoretical dynamic relationship, not a deterministic predictive relationship. Key stock accumulation does not necessarily lead to changes in system conditions, and changes in system conditions do not necessarily produce a new dominant loop. A new loop may still be affected by time, resources, competitor reactions, and other loops.

Therefore, 3A is a necessary condition framework, not a success prediction model. What it attempts to answer is not "whether an innovation will ultimately succeed," but: **Whether a new loop possesses the basic conditions to move from structural differences to self-reinforcement, and further gain system power.**

## 7.5 The Theory Itself as a New Loop: A Meta-Level Test Case

If The New Loop Theory itself attempts to enter the competitive system of business analysis theories, then it can also be observed as a new loop. This is not merely an analogical case, but a meta-level test of the theory's self-consistency: if the new loop mechanism described by The New Loop Theory cannot explain its own growth, then the explanatory boundaries of the theory itself also need to be re-examined.

For The New Loop Theory, its core stock can be defined as **mental model share**. Mental model share here is not equivalent to public awareness, but refers to the irreplaceability of The New Loop Theory's exclusive concepts in the context of business analysis, that is, its explanatory power, and the continuously accumulating high-quality positive and negative example libraries revolving around these concepts.

The true sign of the growth of this stock is not that "more people have heard of The New Loop Theory," but that when certain types of business problems arise, an increasing number of important analysts find that it is difficult to explain the problem with the same precision without using concepts such as stock migration, sequential ascendance, and 3A. When these concepts begin to become linguistic infrastructure that cannot be easily bypassed when analyzing problems, the theory truly begins to accumulate mental model share.

Around this core stock, at least two mutually reinforcing reinforcing loops can be formed.

The first is the **concept propagation loop**. A counter-intuitive concept with penetrating power first breaks through the blind spots that existing theories cannot explain, and then becomes a cognitive tool for KOLs, analysts, investors, and decision-makers to make judgments. Once a concept can generate high judgment returns with very low expression costs, it is easy to become a cognitive social currency, and further generate more derivative content and case breakdowns. More content and usage will bring more cases into the theoretical system, enabling the theory to obtain new explanatory objects, thereby further promoting concept evolution.

_**Reinforcing Loop One: Concept Propagation Loop**_
> Counter-intuitive new concepts → Break through blind spots that old theories cannot explain → Become cognitive tools and social currency for analysts, investors, and decision-makers → Increase in derivative content and cases → Increase in mental model share → More people use these concepts → More cases actively enter the theoretical system

The second is the **reality validation loop**. When theoretical frameworks such as 3A enter real innovation decisions and investment judgments, if they can identify wrong paths, pseudo-flywheels, or system constraints, they may produce direct real-world gains. The real cases, counterexamples, and boundary conditions generated by practitioners from this will in turn enter the theoretical system, promoting the improvement of the theory's explanatory power and falsification precision. The more the theory can handle high-value real problems, the more likely it is to enter higher-quality decision-making scenarios, and further accumulate new mental model share.

_**Reinforcing Loop Two: Reality Validation Loop**_
> Frameworks like 3A enter real decisions → Identify wrong paths, pseudo-flywheels, and system constraints → Produce real decision-making value → Practitioners feedback real cases → Theory obtains new boundary conditions and counterexamples → Theory's explanatory power and falsification precision improve → Attract more high-value decision scenarios → Further increase in mental model share

These two reinforcing loops accumulate the theory's propagation potential and validation potential respectively, but they ultimately point to the same core stock: **irreplaceable explanatory power, and a continuously expanding stock of high-quality cases.**

At the same time, the theory will also face its own balancing loops.

**The first balancing loop** comes from the business world's continuous demand for certainty and success prescriptions. Business communication naturally prefers "must-win rules," "disrupting giants," and quickly replicable success paths, while a truly rigorous theory must emphasize conditions, boundaries, and counterexamples. The more rigorous the theory, the greater the distance it may have from success-oriented narratives, and the momentum of mass communication may therefore be limited.

**The second balancing loop** comes from semantic drift and communication entropy increase. After the theory's popularity increases, concept imitation, misuse, and labeled use will also increase. The expansion of communication scale itself does not necessarily increase theoretical value; it may also lead to core concepts detaching from the original theoretical structure and being repackaged into old analytical frameworks. When concept boundaries continue to blur, the theory's explanatory power will decline, and its core stock will also be eroded.

Therefore, the theory needs to establish feedback mechanisms to protect its own boundaries, including strict terminology definitions, 3A judgment criteria, counterexample mechanisms, logical constraints, and the active exclusion of cases that do not meet theoretical conditions. What these mechanisms protect is not the theory's sense of scarcity, but its **boundary integrity**. Only when the core concepts of the theory still maintain sufficiently stable structural meanings during the communication process can the newly generated usage produced by communication continue to transform into true theoretical stock.

**The third balancing loop** comes from the ecological resistance formed by existing analytical frameworks. Traditional strategic consulting, business schools, and other existing analytical systems all possess long-accumulated knowledge stocks, usage habits, and interest relationships. After a new analytical loop enters, the existing system may reduce its structural differences through absorption, rewriting, grafting, and labeled use, or it may directly form ecological rejection. This means that the growth of a new theory is not only a communication issue, but also a competition between a new value accumulation path and an existing value accumulation path.

In this meta-level case, net amplifying should therefore not be understood as a simple growth rate calculation. It describes whether the reinforcing loops within the theory have sufficient structural potential to enable the core stock to continue accumulating in the presence of continuous balancing forces such as the demand for successology, semantic drift, and existing ecological resistance.

This also means that the mark of a theory truly forming self-amplifying capabilities is not that it has more and more followers, but that it begins to produce a deeper dependency relationship: **the more high-quality problems it explains, the more high-quality cases enter its boundaries, the more high-value decision-makers use it to make judgments, the harder it is for other existing frameworks to replace it in the same way.**

If this process continues to occur, the theory itself may also experience evolution from an edge loop to a dominant loop. At that time, The New Loop Theory will no longer just be a theoretical object explaining business systems, but will become an actual participant in the business analysis system, and will begin to change the way other analytical frameworks understand innovation problems.

This constitutes one of the most important meta-level tests of The New Loop Theory:

> **A theory that explains how a new loop forms must itself be able to form, accumulate, and change the system it is in as a new loop.**

# 8. Stock Migration Mechanism of New Loops

In complex business systems, the formation of a new loop does not always mean starting from scratch to build a completely independent value creation system. In fact, a large number of successful new loops are not born outside the system, but find new value accumulation methods within the existing system and reorganize original resources to serve different value logics.

This means we need to correct a linear innovation assumption that is easy to produce: innovators do not always have to create new resources, but under specific conditions, they can rediscover the value of existing resources in the new system.

After long-term operation, a mature business system will precipitate a large amount of resources. These resources may include user relationships, technological capabilities, brand trust, supply chain networks, transaction data, or organizational experience. They initially served the old loop, so their value was defined by the gain function of the old loop. But when a new value creation method emerges, some existing resources may detach from the original cycle and enter a new value creation structure.

This phenomenon is called **Stock Migration** in The New Loop Theory.

Stock migration refers to resources already formed in the existing system being reorganized under a new value creation logic and entering a new value cycle.

The key to stock migration is not "having more resources," but whether resources can change the possibilities for the formation and development of the new loop.

## 8.1 From Resource Inheritance to Value Reconstruction

Traditional competitive analysis often focuses on how many resources an enterprise has and whether these resources constitute a competitive advantage. But in a dynamic business system, resources themselves do not directly produce advantages. The same resource may generate completely different value in different loops.

For example, a platform with a large number of users can use user scale for ad distribution, or for transaction matchmaking, content recommendation, or ecosystem building. User scale itself does not determine the final value; what truly determines the competitive result is: into which feedback structure are these resources connected?

Therefore, stock migration is not asset transfer in a simple sense (old business resources → new business replication),
but rather: **Existing stocks → Reorganization → New value cycle**

Only when resources enter a new cycle and change the way value is accumulated in the cycle is true migration completed.

This is also why many enterprises have massive historical accumulation but cannot complete the second growth curve. They have resources but cannot change the role of resources in the system; they inherit past advantages but cannot transform these advantages into new growth drivers.

## 8.2 Stock Migration and the Formation of New Loops

In the process of new loop formation, stock migration provides an important path: innovators do not necessarily need to challenge the existing dominant players head-on in the entire business system, but can find value nodes that have not been fully released in the existing competitive system. This reduces the cost of establishing the new loop in the early stage.

A completely new loop starting from scratch needs to simultaneously complete: value logic validation, user relationship establishment, resource accumulation, and market education. Stock migration allows innovators to leverage part of the accumulation in the existing system to accelerate the formation of the new loop.

However, it should be noted that stock migration does not mean that a new business inheriting the advantages of the old loop will definitely succeed. If the migrated resources still operate in the way of the old loop, then it is just business expansion, not the formation of a new loop.

True stock migration must satisfy a core condition: **The migrated resources must serve the new direction of value accumulation, rather than continuing to reinforce the old loop.**

## 8.3 Case Observation: Xiaomi - From MIUI to the Ecosystem
> _**Nature of Case Observation:** This case is presented solely as an illustrative observation of the underlying theoretical mechanisms, intended to concretize the abstract concepts within the New Loop Theory. The case details are drawn from publicly available business histories and industry consensus, and should not be construed as rigorous business historiography or empirical academic research._

Xiaomi's early development process embodies a typical form of stock migration. In the early stage of the smartphone industry, the competitive loop of traditional mobile phone manufacturers mainly revolved around hardware capabilities:

**Product R&D → Channel Sales → Phone Sales → Next-generation Product Investment**

In this loop, users were more the endpoint of product sales, rather than co-participants in the product iteration process.

Xiaomi did not initially challenge the entire mobile phone industry chain directly, but sought a new value accumulation entry point within the Android ecosystem. The MIUI community connected user feedback, product iteration, and user participation, transforming user relationships from one-time transactional relationships to continuous participatory relationships.

In this process, Xiaomi did not create the resource of "user feedback," nor did it create the Android ecosystem itself. Instead, it reorganized a node in the existing ecosystem to form a new cycle:

**User Participation → Product Feedback → System Optimization → User Growth → More User Participation**

When this cycle gradually matured, it further migrated into a larger commercial system, connecting with mobile phone hardware, IoT devices, and ecosystem chain enterprises to form a more complex value network. The Xiaomi case illustrates that a new loop does not necessarily have to enter from outside the old loop system. An organization can cultivate new growth directions within the existing competitive system by redefining the value roles of existing resources.

## 8.4 Theoretical Significance of Stock Migration

Stock migration supplements The New Loop Theory's understanding of "newness."

The "new" in a new loop does not mean that all constituent elements must be new. What truly matters is: Has the value relationship changed?

A new loop may use users, technologies, channels, or even infrastructure from the existing business system, but if these resources are reconnected and enter a different feedback structure, it may still form a new value creation logic.

Therefore, to judge whether an innovation belongs to a new loop, one cannot simply observe whether it has established new stock resources; it is also necessary to observe: Have existing stock resources been reorganized and served for different value accumulation directions?

Stock migration explains why some enterprises can achieve breakthroughs in mature markets, and also explains why some enterprises with huge resource advantages cannot establish new growth cycles: resources do not migrate automatically; only when resources enter a new loop can they generate new value.

# 9. First Principles and New Loops

## 9.1 What First Principles Answer

First principles help us return to more basic facts of things and re-examine those assumptions that have been taken for granted in a field.

It can help innovators discover that the product forms, business models, and competition methods we take for granted today are not necessarily the inevitable results of the problem itself.

But discovering underlying facts does not automatically mean discovering a new business loop.

First principles help answer what is fundamentally possible, while The New Loop Theory further asks: "Based on these underlying facts, can a different value creation and accumulation mechanism be formed?"

## 9.2 From Essence to Loops

Starting from first principles, innovators may discover an underlying capability that was previously overlooked.

But only when this capability is embedded in new causal relationships and begins to generate continuous feedback can it become part of a new loop.

Therefore, first principles can be an important entry point for discovering new loops, but they cannot replace the new loop itself.

## 9.3 NVIDIA CUDA

GPUs originally possessed highly parallel computing capabilities. One of the truly important changes was that NVIDIA, through CUDA, turned this computing capability into a general-purpose computing foundation that is easier for developers to use and program, and gradually formed a continuous relationship among developers, software ecosystems, application demands, and computing capabilities.

Two different levels of questions can be seen here. Recognizing the parallel computing capability of GPUs is a re-recognition of underlying capabilities; while the developer and software ecosystem built around CUDA has enabled this capability to enter a new value creation and accumulation relationship.

Therefore, what this case demonstrates is not "first principles equal new loops," but how underlying facts become the foundation for the formation of new loops.

# 11. New Loops and Moats

Moats are usually used to describe an enterprise's ability to maintain a competitive advantage over the long term.

From the perspective of The New Loop Theory, a moat is not a static asset unrelated to loops. Truly powerful moats often come from stocks formed after long-term operation and the mutual reinforcement among these stocks.

Users, data, brands, technological capabilities, channels, and ecosystem partners can all become part of a moat, but the reason they form protection is that they are embedded in a continuously operating value loop.

This also means that moats are not permanently unchanging.

When a new loop changes system conditions, the stocks corresponding to the old moat may still exist, but their gain functions change. Resources that could form strong protection in the past may no longer have the same value in the new system.

From this perspective, what truly needs attention is not "whether an enterprise has a moat," but: **Which loop is this moat attached to, and are the system conditions of this loop changing?**

# 12. Theoretical Boundaries

The New Loop Theory must first know its theoretical boundaries.

Not all innovations belong to the "new loops" described by The New Loop Theory. A product can be better, faster, cheaper, have brand new functions, or bring significant user growth, but as long as it still operates within the original value creation and accumulation logic, it cannot be classified into a new loop merely based on being "new."

## 12.1 Compared with Incremental Innovation

Incremental innovation is usually continuous improvement on existing products, capabilities, and competitive logic. It can be extremely valuable and produce huge business results.

But if the improvement does not form a different value creation and accumulation loop, it still belongs to innovation within the old loop.

The New Loop Theory focuses on changes in loop structure, not on how large the magnitude of change is.

## 12.2 What is Not The New Loop

New technologies, new products, new functions, new business models, or even rapid growth cannot independently prove that the "new loop" focused on by The New Loop Theory is established.

The key to judgment remains: Whether a structurally different value creation and accumulation mechanism has been formed, and whether it has begun to establish its own feedback relationships.

## 12.3 Why Not All New Products are New Loops

A product is a result that users can directly observe, while a loop describes the system relationship that produces and accumulates this result.

Two products can have completely different appearances yet operate in the same loop; the same product may also gradually enter another loop as the business model and system conditions change.

Therefore, product innovation and new loop innovation are not concepts on the same level.

## 12.4 Why Growth Does Not Mean Ascendant

Growth only describes changes in scale.

**Ascendant** in the 3A framework describes the change in the system's dominant value accumulation path. A new product can grow at high speed yet still be attached to the old loop; a business may also grow very fast in a local market but has not formed a force capable of changing the system's attractor.

Therefore, growth itself is insufficient to prove being ascendant.

## 12.5 Why Subsidized Growth Does Not Mean Amplifying

External capital, price subsidies, resource tilt, or artificial promotion can all manufacture growth.

**Amplifying** in the 3A framework focuses on whether there is a feedback structure within the new loop that can continuously reinforce itself.

Therefore, growth driven by external resources cannot be directly used as proof of the existence of a self-amplifying loop.

# 13. Relationship with Related Existing Theories

The New Loop Theory has obvious connections with multiple existing innovation and strategy theories, but the problems studied are not exactly the same.

**Christensen's "Disruptive Innovation"** mainly explains how some new entrants enter from specific market positions and change the competitive structure under specific conditions. The New Loop Theory further focuses on how the value creation loop itself forms, and how the new loop changes system conditions.

**First Principles** help innovators return to more underlying facts and assumptions. The New Loop Theory pushes this underlying understanding further forward to value generation and feedback structures.

**Lean Startup** focuses on how to reduce the verification cost of innovation assumptions. The New Loop Theory asks a step earlier: What direction is worth being verified, and what value relationships may form a new loop?

**Design Thinking** emphasizes user needs, experience, and value creation. The New Loop Theory focuses on whether these value relationships can further form different system feedback.

**The Second Curve** emphasizes new growth curves outside the existing growth curve. The New Loop Theory attempts to explain the causal structure behind this new curve, and the system relationship between the old and new loops.

**Strategy Theories** focus on how enterprises choose competitive fields, establish unique positions, and gain long-term advantages. The New Loop Theory pushes the problem further to the loop level where competitive advantages are produced: In what loop is the enterprise competing, and is there another loop worth entering?

These theories can complement each other, but they are not different expressions of the same theoretical problem.

# 14. Areas of Focus and Theoretical Limitations

## 14.1 Not an Organizational Change Theory

The "new loop" focused on by The New Loop Theory may require the organization to change, but The New Loop Theory itself does not explain how the organization should be designed, governed, or how organizational change should be implemented.

The organization is just an important system condition in which the new loop operates.

## 14.2 Not a Predictive Model

The New Loop Theory describes a kind of system innovation pattern and its possible dynamic evolution, but it is not a predictive model.

Even if a new loop possesses certain theoretical conditions, it cannot be used to calculate when it will succeed, what the probability of success is, or that it will definitely become the market leader.

## 14.3 Cannot Eliminate Uncertainty in Complex Systems

Complex adaptive systems have a large number of interacting and incompletely controllable factors.

New loops can change the system, but innovators cannot completely control how the system responds. Therefore, the theory can help understand the structure, but cannot eliminate uncertainty.

## 14.4 Not a Unified Explanation for All Innovations

The New Loop Theory only explains specific types of structural innovation.

A large number of product optimizations, incremental innovations, technological improvements, operational innovations, and organizational innovations do not need to be explained as new loops.

The value of the theory lies not in covering all innovations, but in accurately explaining the specific class of phenomena it has chosen.

## 14.5 Does Not Guarantee the Success of New Loops

There are multiple stages between the formation, growth, and system substitution of a new loop.

Even if a new loop has structural differences and forms a self-amplifying mechanism, it may still fail to become the new dominant path due to insufficient resources, immature timing, changes in the external environment, competitor reactions, or the emergence of other stronger loops.

Therefore, The New Loop Theory explains a possible mechanism of system evolution, not a guarantee of success.
