# Abstract

The New Loop Theory is a theoretical framework for understanding the evolution of commercial systems. It seeks to explain a longstanding question in innovation and strategy: why do some emerging forces that begin at the margins eventually change the way an entire competitive system works, causing companies, resources, and capabilities once regarded as decisive advantages to lose their dominant position?

Unlike conventional competitive analysis, The New Loop Theory does not begin with direct comparisons between firms, nor does it primarily ask who can defeat whom. It focuses on a deeper question: how is value generated, accumulated, and redistributed within a commercial system? What ultimately determines long-term competitive outcomes—the advantages a company possesses, or the system structure that continuously produces those advantages?

The theory views the commercial environment as a Complex Adaptive System and proposes the **Loop** as the fundamental unit of analysis for understanding commercial evolution. From this perspective, competitive advantage does not simply reside in a product, technology, resource, or brand. It is continuously produced by a set of feedback relationships operating under particular system conditions.

A mature commercial system is typically sustained by an established **Old Loop**. Through accumulated resources, capabilities, and organizational adaptation, the Old Loop develops a stable competitive advantage. Yet a new way of creating value may emerge at the edge of the system and gradually establish a different logic of value generation. Once the **New Loop** develops its own reinforcing mechanisms and accumulates sufficient momentum, the flow of value within the system may begin to shift, eventually changing the structure of competition itself.

The New Loop Theory identifies three core conditions for describing the evolutionary potential of a New Loop: **Asymmetric**, referring to a structurally different value-generation logic; **Amplifying**, referring to an endogenous reinforcing mechanism; and **Ascendant**, referring to the emergence of a new dominant path of value accumulation at the system level. Together, they form a causal chain from the formation of a New Loop to systemic change.

> **The New Loop Theory is not primarily about how a company defeats an old system. It is about how a new system emerges, causing the advantages on which the old system depends to gradually lose their dominance.**

The current version is not intended to be a predictive model, nor does it provide a set of innovation tools. It addresses a more fundamental theoretical question:

**Under what structural conditions can a New Loop emerge within an existing commercial system and ultimately change the direction of value accumulation?**

# 1. The Problem

## 1.1 The Semantic Drift of “Disruptive Innovation”

Among the most influential concepts in modern innovation theory is **Disruptive Innovation**. Yet as the concept has traveled across languages and contexts, the Chinese term commonly used to translate “disruptive” has acquired a stronger connotation than the original theoretical concept necessarily implies.

In everyday Chinese usage, the equivalent of “disruption” often suggests overthrowing, defeating, replacing, or even destroying something that already exists. This linguistic shift has encouraged a particular mental model of innovation: innovation means finding a way to challenge an incumbent and eventually defeat it.

That interpretation naturally frames innovation as a direct confrontation:

> The startup challenges the incumbent. The newcomer defeats the leader. The new model replaces the old one.

Once innovation is understood as “finding a way to beat the giant,” attention inevitably turns toward the competitor: where to attack, how to outperform, and how to prove that one’s product is better.

This reinforces a confrontational narrative around innovation while obscuring a more fundamental question embedded in the original body of innovation research:

**Why can some innovations that initially appear marginal, weak, or insignificant eventually change the structure of an entire market?**

Many forces that reshape industries do not begin by directly confronting the established system. Instead, they often develop a different mechanism for creating value outside the areas where the incumbent system is paying the most attention. As the new mechanism grows, it gradually changes the competitive environment around it.

The New Loop Theory is therefore neither a renaming of Disruptive Innovation nor an attempt to replace existing innovation theories.

It asks a more fundamental question: if innovation is not fundamentally about confrontation, what mechanism actually drives systemic change?

And more specifically:

**What kind of New Loop can emerge within an existing system, accumulate strength, and eventually change the system itself?**

## 1.2 From Competitive Outcomes to System Evolution

Traditional business analysis often begins with comparisons between firms:

* Who has better technology?
* Who has more users?
* Who has greater market share?
* Who operates more efficiently?

These questions focus on competitive outcomes. But a commercial system is not a static chessboard.

Every decision made by a company changes resource allocation. Resource allocation changes organizational capabilities. Capabilities shape products and services. Products and services influence user behavior. User behavior, in turn, affects revenue, data, brand, and ecosystem relationships. These changes are interconnected and continuously feed back into the system itself.

A company's advantage today, therefore, may not be the result of any single isolated factor.

The more important question is:

**Why do these factors continuously reinforce one another and keep producing advantage?**

From this perspective, users, brands, channels, technology, and organizational capabilities are all forms of **Stock** within the system. What matters more is how these stocks are connected by loops, and under what conditions those loops can continue to generate value.

A mature company's market, customers, channels, supply chain, organizational capabilities, technological capabilities, and capital resources have typically developed mutually reinforcing relationships through years of competition. These relationships are more than a collection of advantages the company happens to possess. Together, they constitute the way the company continuously creates value.

As a value-generation mechanism operates over a long period of time, resource allocation, organizational capabilities, and performance criteria increasingly adapt to it. A system that once enabled a company to succeed can therefore gradually constrain its ability to discover another way of creating value.

This helps explain a longstanding phenomenon: successful companies are not necessarily the ones best positioned to create their own substitutes. The problem is not simply that organizations “fail to innovate.” The Old Loop itself continuously reinforces the dimensions of competition that the organization already knows how to optimize.

## 1.3 What the Theory Seeks to Explain

The New Loop Theory seeks to explain a particular kind of change.

A new way of creating value may begin very small, even outside the mainstream market. It does not immediately defeat established firms or capture the largest market share. Instead, it begins to establish its own feedback relationships.

As those feedback relationships continue, the New Loop gradually accumulates users, data, capabilities, resources, or ecosystem relationships. These new stocks then strengthen the loop itself, giving it increasing capacity for self-reinforcement.

At the same time, the conditions of the broader system begin to change. Once those changes accumulate sufficiently, the advantages that previously sustained the Old Loop may no longer generate the same value. The old system does not necessarily disappear overnight. What changes is the way in which it generates value.

The New Loop Theory is concerned with this process and with a related set of questions:

Why do some innovations that initially appear marginal gradually develop their own growth mechanism?

Why does having users not necessarily mean that a new product has established a new competitive logic?

Why can the advantages accumulated by an established system lose their former value when system conditions change?

And, most importantly:

**Under what conditions can a local New Loop evolve into a new dominant loop at the system level?**

These questions define the research object of The New Loop Theory.

# 2. Core Proposition

## 2.1 The Commercial Environment as a Complex Adaptive System

A commercial environment is not simply a collection of static variables. Companies, users, suppliers, channels, technologies, capital, and institutions interact continuously, and each action changes the conditions under which other participants will act in the future.

As a result, outcomes in a commercial system are rarely determined by a single factor. They emerge from multiple feedback relationships operating over time.

From the perspective of a Complex Adaptive System, competitive advantage is therefore not simply an attribute that a company “has.” It is better understood as an outcome continuously produced by the system under particular conditions.

A company may possess technological capabilities, a brand, users, channels, or data. These are all forms of Stock. But the value those stocks can generate depends on the conditions in which they operate and on the loops through which they are connected.

## 2.2 Old Loop and New Loop

The **Old Loop** is the value-generation and accumulation mechanism that currently occupies a dominant position within a system. It is typically composed of a set of mutually reinforcing causal relationships and has, through prolonged competition, produced stable patterns of resource allocation.

The **New Loop** is a value-generation and accumulation mechanism that differs structurally from the Old Loop. It may emerge at the edge of an existing system, or it may originate from a condition that has been overlooked within the system itself.

The New Loop does not need to be larger than the Old Loop at the beginning. What matters is that it starts generating value through a different causal logic and begins to accumulate value through that logic.

The meaningful comparison between a New Loop and an Old Loop therefore does not begin with product features or isolated advantages. It begins with **how value is generated, reinforced, and accumulated**.

## 2.3 What “Replacement” Means

When The New Loop Theory refers to “replacement,” it does not mean that an old company, product, or capability must disappear.

An Old Loop can continue to exist and may retain commercial value for a long time. What changes is the way that Old Loop generates value within the system.

As the New Loop changes system conditions, the stocks accumulated by the Old Loop may remain in place while generating a different level or type of value than before. The Old Loop consequently loses some of its former reinforcing power, while the New Loop begins to provide a more stable path for value accumulation.

Replacement therefore appears first as a **shift in the dominant path of value accumulation**, rather than as a simple change in market share.

## 2.4 Core Definition

> **The New Loop Theory studies how new value-generation and accumulation loops emerge within existing competitive systems and, through self-reinforcement, stock accumulation, and changes in system conditions, may ultimately become a new dominant path of value accumulation.**

# 3. What “New Loop” Means by “Descending”

“Descending” does not mean simplifying a complex problem or reducing it to a few easy steps.

It refers to moving downward in the level at which we observe a phenomenon.

We usually encounter a business problem first as an event: a product performs better, an industry changes, or a customer begins adopting a new solution. With further observation, we may see that these events form recurring patterns. We can then ask what structures generate those patterns, and eventually examine the assumptions and mental models that sustain those structures.

The observation path can therefore be expressed as:

**Event → Pattern → Structure → Mental Model**

Descend from events to structures; from structures, look for new possibilities.

Important innovation opportunities do not always exist within the competitive dimension that current players are fighting over. Sometimes they exist within the deeper structure that produces that competitive dimension in the first place.

The New Loop Theory therefore does not ask innovators simply to “think bigger.” It asks them to reconsider why the current system generates value in the way it does, and whether changing a deeper causal relationship could produce a different loop.

# 4. The Loop: The Fundamental Unit of Analysis

## 4.1 What Is a Loop?

In The New Loop Theory, a Loop is not a rhetorical way of describing something that “goes around.”

It refers to a causal structure in which a set of factors influence one another and form a feedback relationship. When an outcome feeds back into the conditions that produced it, further reinforcing or counteracting the original change, a feedback loop emerges.

The defining characteristic of a Loop is therefore not the number of factors involved, but the existence of a sustained causal feedback structure among them.

From this perspective, competitive advantage can be understood differently. Advantage is not simply a resource a company possesses. It can emerge from a Loop in which resources, behaviors, and outcomes continuously reinforce one another and sustain value creation.

## 4.2 Reinforcing Loops

A reinforcing loop causes change to continue in the same direction.

For example, more users can generate more feedback; that feedback can improve the product; a better product can increase user value; and higher user value can attract more users. What matters here is not simply “user growth,” but the reinforcing causal relationship between user growth and product improvement.

Growth itself therefore does not prove that a reinforcing loop exists.

A company may grow rapidly through capital investment, subsidies, or external resources. If that growth does not create an internal feedback structure that strengthens the system itself, growth should not be equated with **Amplifying**.

## 4.3 Balancing Loops

A balancing loop counteracts change and pushes a system toward a relatively stable state.

In mature organizations, processes, rules, risk controls, resource allocation mechanisms, and evaluation systems may all perform this function. They help preserve stability and make established systems more persistent.

But balancing loops are not the only source of organizational rigidity. Resource specialization, path dependence, existing interests, organizational structures, and evaluation systems can further reinforce an established system.

Understanding the stability of an Old Loop therefore requires these factors to be viewed as part of the larger system.

## 4.4 Stock

Stock refers to a state or resource accumulated through past activity that can influence future system behavior.

Users, data, brand, technological capabilities, channels, organizational capabilities, ecosystem partners, and capital resources can all function as Stock.

Stock itself is not synonymous with competitive advantage. Its value is realized through the Loop in which it operates.

This distinction is central to The New Loop Theory: a New Loop does not necessarily require the existing Stock of the old system to disappear. It may instead change the system conditions under which that Stock generates value.

## 4.5 Gain Function

The same Stock can generate very different levels of value under different system conditions.

The value or competitive gain generated by a given Stock under specific system conditions can therefore be understood as its **Gain Function**.

It can be represented simply as:

**Gain_old(S, C₀) ≠ Gain_old(S, C₁)**

where **S** represents existing Stock and **C** represents system conditions.

This means that a resource that was once extremely powerful does not automatically retain the same value in the future. One of the important effects of a New Loop is to change system conditions in ways that alter the Gain Function of existing Stock.

This provides an important entry point for understanding why an established advantage can lose its former value.

## 4.6 Attractor

Complex systems do not move indefinitely in every possible direction. Through long-term feedback, they tend to converge toward certain relatively stable states or paths. These can be understood as **Attractors**.

In a commercial system, an Attractor can be understood as a dominant long-term path of value accumulation.

The **Ascendant** status of a New Loop therefore does not simply mean becoming “number one” in the market. It means that the New Loop begins to become a more important and more stable path through which value accumulates across the system.

This is why The New Loop Theory is ultimately concerned not only with the sales of a particular product, but with the direction in which the system itself is converging.

# 5. From Competitive Advantage to Competitive Loops

## 5.1 How Advantage Is Continuously Produced by a System

Traditional competitive analysis often treats advantage as an attribute a company possesses: cost, brand, technology, distribution, scale, or something similar.

From a systems perspective, however, these advantages are rarely static.

Scale can create cost advantages; lower costs can attract more customers; more customers can create greater scale. Users can generate data; data can improve the product; product improvement can attract more users.

Advantage is therefore often the outcome of a Loop operating over time.

## 5.2 Loop vs. Loop

If competition is primarily a comparison of resources between firms, the central innovation question becomes “Who has more?”

But if competition takes place between Loops, the question changes.

A participant with fewer resources may be able to avoid entering the established competitive dimension by creating a different value-generation Loop. The early scale of the New Loop may be very small because what it needs to establish first is its own feedback structure.

Innovation therefore becomes less about comparing who possesses stronger existing advantages and more about asking:

**Which Loop can create stronger value accumulation over time?**

## 5.3 The System Dependence of Advantage

The value of an advantage depends on the system in which it operates.

A Stock that is extremely important within an Old Loop does not necessarily produce the same gain under different system conditions. Conversely, a resource previously regarded as unimportant can become highly valuable when the system changes.

One of the deeper opportunities for innovation therefore lies in changing what is valuable and why it is valuable.

This is a fundamental reason why a New Loop can change the structure of competition.

# 6. When the Old Loop Becomes a Constraint on Innovation

## 6.1 Stabilization of the Loop

When a Loop operates for a long period of time, it produces increasing amounts of resources and capabilities that are aligned with itself.

Organizations recruit, evaluate, invest, and allocate resources according to the Loop. Customers and channels gradually adapt to the same way of doing business.

The Old Loop therefore becomes more than a mechanism for creating value. It begins to shape the organization and the market around it.

## 6.2 The Rigidifying Effect of Balancing Loops

Stable systems need mechanisms that suppress deviation.

New ideas often introduce uncertainty, require resource reallocation, and may reduce short-term performance against existing metrics. Mature systems therefore naturally develop balancing mechanisms.

These mechanisms are not inherently problematic. They are necessary for organizational stability.

The difficulty arises when a system needs to explore another Loop and the mechanisms designed to preserve stability begin to suppress the formation of that new Loop.

## 6.3 Why Successful Systems Struggle to Create Their Own Substitutes

A successful system is usually highly capable of continuing to operate the Loop that has already been validated.

It possesses capabilities, resources, processes, and evaluation criteria that are closely aligned with the Old Loop. Any attempt to move outside that Loop therefore carries a higher internal cost of justification.

Success can consequently create a structural constraint of its own: the better a system becomes at optimizing its current Loop, the harder it may become to establish another Loop outside it.

This does not mean that mature companies cannot innovate. It means that another Loop often needs room to develop at the edge of the existing system or within a different logic of resource allocation.

# 7. The 3A Constraint Framework

The New Loop Theory uses three conditions to describe the fundamental constraints a New Loop faces as it develops from an emerging mechanism into a systemic force: **Asymmetric, Amplifying, and Ascendant**.

These are not three independent labels. They correspond to three different theoretical questions.

## 7.1 Asymmetric

**Asymmetric** means that the New Loop differs structurally from the Old Loop in its value-generation logic, revenue formation, or competitive Gain Function.

The difference cannot be reduced to having more features, a lower price, or a better experience. A product can be substantially better while still operating within the Old Loop.

The essential question is:

**If the New Loop works, does it generate and accumulate value through a different causal logic from the Old Loop?**

Asymmetry is therefore a structural requirement for something to qualify as genuinely new.

## 7.2 Amplifying

**Amplifying** means that the New Loop contains an endogenous reinforcing mechanism.

An outcome produced within the New Loop feeds back and strengthens other conditions within the Loop, allowing the Loop to become stronger as it operates.

Amplifying therefore describes a causal structure rather than a growth outcome.

A system that depends on continuous external capital, subsidies, or manual resources may grow rapidly, but that growth alone does not demonstrate an Amplifying mechanism.

## 7.3 Ascendant

**Ascendant** describes a system-level outcome.

When a New Loop develops over time, begins to change system conditions, and gradually becomes a more important path of value accumulation within the system, it can be said to have become Ascendant.

Ascendant therefore does not simply mean “winning the market,” nor is it a static market-share metric. It reflects a shift in the system's Attractor.

## 7.4 The Causal Chain

The three conditions can be understood through a basic dynamic relationship:

**Asymmetric → Amplifying → Stock Accumulation → System Condition Change → Attractor Shift → Ascendant**

It is important to emphasize that 3A is a framework of necessary conditions, not a predictive model of success. Even a New Loop with structural asymmetry and endogenous reinforcement may still be constrained by time, resources, external conditions, competitor responses, and other Loops.

# 8. How a New Loop Becomes the New Dominant Loop

A New Loop does not become the dominant Loop simply because it has been discovered. It must develop through a dynamic process. This process should not be understood as a rigid linear sequence, nor will every innovation pass through all of its stages. It nevertheless describes a typical evolutionary path.

A New Loop often begins at the edge of an established system. It may serve people that the mainstream market has overlooked, exploit a resource that previously seemed unimportant, or establish a value relationship that differs from the dominant competitive logic. At this stage, scale is not the most important variable. What matters is that the New Loop begins to operate independently.

Once the New Loop develops reinforcing relationships, it begins to accumulate its own Stock. Users, data, technological capabilities, brand, channels, ecosystem relationships, and organizational capabilities may all accumulate as the Loop operates. These stocks, in turn, increase the New Loop's capacity to continue operating and expanding.

As Stock accumulates, the New Loop begins to acquire momentum. It may still be a local phenomenon within the larger system, but its effects increasingly extend beyond its own boundaries and begin to alter the conditions under which other participants operate.

As these changes reach a sufficient level, the competitive dimension itself may begin to shift. Conditions that previously determined advantage become influenced by the new system conditions, while the conditions on which the New Loop depends become increasingly important.

At this point, the central mechanism of The New Loop Theory begins to emerge:

**New Loop Formation → New Value Accumulation → System Condition Change → Change in the Gain Function of Existing Stock → Declining Reinforcement of the Old Loop → Attractor Shift**

The decline of the Old Loop does not require all of its assets to disappear. Stores, brands, channels, equipment, organizational capabilities, and even customers may continue to exist.

What changes is the relationship between those stocks and the system conditions under which they generate value.

When existing Stock no longer produces the same gain under the new conditions, it gradually loses its ability to reinforce the Old Loop. Meanwhile, the New Loop continues to accumulate Stock and reinforce itself. The system therefore begins to converge toward a different path of value accumulation.

That is what Ascendant describes: the New Loop is no longer merely a local innovation. It is becoming the system's new dominant path of value accumulation.

# 9. First Principles and the New Loop

## 9.1 What First Principles Answers

First Principles is an important entry point for The New Loop Theory.

It begins with the question:

> **What is this thing at its most fundamental level?**

It helps us move beyond industry conventions, historical assumptions, and established categories to examine the basic properties of an object or system.

But identifying a fundamental property does not, by itself, reveal a New Loop. A fundamental property becomes commercially significant only when it is connected to users, technology, resources, ecosystems, or other system conditions in a way that creates sustained value feedback.

## 9.2 From Fundamental Properties to Loops

The critical step between First Principles and New Loop thinking is therefore the transformation of an underlying property into a new causal relationship.

First Principles can reveal possibilities that were hidden by established assumptions. The New Loop Theory then asks whether those possibilities can form a new mechanism for generating and accumulating value.

This is also where the two concepts diverge.

First Principles can help discover a New Loop, but First Principles itself is not the New Loop Theory.

## 9.3 The NVIDIA CUDA Case

From a conventional competitive perspective, GPU manufacturers might primarily compete around gaming performance, graphics processing, and hardware specifications.

Recognizing the highly parallel computational capability of GPUs opened possibilities beyond traditional graphics processing. CUDA then made that capability programmable and accessible to developers, gradually creating a reinforcing ecosystem among developers, software applications, computing demand, and GPUs.

The underlying dynamic can be expressed as:

**GPU Parallel Computing Capability → CUDA → Developer Ecosystem → Software Applications → Computing Demand → GPU Demand**

The theoretical significance is not simply that “GPUs can also compute.” It is that an underlying capability was connected into a new value-generating Loop.

# 10. A Unified Framework for the Cases

## 10.1 Netflix

The traditional physical rental business could generate value through a Loop built around stores, inventory, geographic location, and rental revenue.

Netflix's early DVD-by-mail model did not simply reproduce this structure. It developed a different value relationship around customer convenience, satisfaction, subscription growth, and scale:

**DVD-by-Mail → Greater Convenience → Higher Satisfaction → Subscription Growth → Scale → Lower Cost**

Netflix later entered streaming, creating a subsequent evolution of its Loop.

The fundamental change facing Blockbuster was therefore not simply that Netflix had a “better store model.” Rather:

> **The conditions that made the physical rental Loop valuable were gradually losing their necessity.**

The Stock of the old system did not immediately disappear. What changed was the relationship between that Stock and value generation.

## 10.2 Xiaomi

The Xiaomi case should not be reduced to competition between smartphone products.

Its value-generation mechanism connected MIUI, user communities, user participation, product feedback, and ecosystem expansion:

**MIUI → User Community → User Participation → Product Feedback → User Stickiness → Ecosystem Expansion**

The important change was that users were not merely end consumers of the product. They became part of the Loop through which products evolved and the ecosystem expanded.

The theoretical significance therefore lies not in whether Xiaomi possessed a particular product capability that exceeded those of competitors, but in how it reorganized the relationships through which value was generated and accumulated.

# 11. The New Loop and the Moat

A **Moat** is not necessarily a prerequisite for a successful business model. It can instead emerge as a consequence of a New Loop operating over time.

Once a New Loop develops a reinforcing mechanism, it continuously accumulates users, data, ecosystem relationships, capabilities, and resources. As these stocks further strengthen the Loop, competitors attempting to reproduce the same outcome may need to enter the same underlying Loop structure.

Path dependence, economies of scale, ecosystem effects, and other structural advantages can therefore emerge over time.

A typical relationship can be expressed as:

**New Loop → Reinforcing Loop → Stock Growth → Further Loop Reinforcement → Path Dependence / Scale Effects / Ecosystem Effects → Defensive Structure**

A Moat is therefore closer to a structural outcome of a New Loop operating over time than to a starting condition used to determine whether an innovation qualifies as New Loop innovation.

# 12. Boundary Conditions

The New Loop Theory does not attempt to include every form of innovation within its explanatory scope. Establishing clear boundaries is essential if the theory is to avoid becoming a framework capable of explaining everything retrospectively.

## 12.1 Incremental Innovation

Incremental innovation is fundamental to business operations. It often occurs within an existing Loop by reducing cost, improving efficiency, enhancing user experience, or increasing performance.

The New Loop Theory focuses on a different type of change: the emergence of a new Loop with its own reinforcing mechanism.

Improving product performance, reducing cost, or enhancing experience therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate the existence of a New Loop. The central question remains whether the causal relationships through which value is generated and accumulated have changed structurally.

## 12.2 What Is Not New Loop Innovation

Not every new product, new technology, new business model, or rapidly growing company represents New Loop innovation.

A new product can be highly successful while still operating within the Old Loop. A company can grow rapidly while relying primarily on capital, subsidies, or external resources. A technology can be highly advanced without creating a new mechanism for accumulating value.

The New Loop Theory therefore examines the Loop, rather than treating “newness” itself as sufficient evidence.

## 12.3 Why Not Every New Product Is a New Loop

A product is an object within a system; a Loop is a causal structure through which value is generated and accumulated.

The same product can potentially operate within different Loops. Creating a different product therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate a change in the competitive structure.

The relevant question is whether the product establishes a value-generation relationship that differs from the Old Loop and whether that relationship can develop its own feedback and accumulation mechanism.

## 12.4 Why Growth Does Not Equal Ascendant

Growth describes an increase in a variable over time. Ascendant describes a change in the direction of system evolution.

A product can acquire millions of users. A company can generate substantial revenue. A business can even become temporarily dominant in market share. Yet these changes may still occur within the Old Loop.

Ascendant describes the point at which the New Loop begins to change the direction of value accumulation and becomes a more important path through which value accumulates across the system.

## 12.5 Why Subsidized Growth Does Not Equal Amplifying

External capital, subsidies, resource allocation, or labor expansion can all produce growth.

But if growth does not create internal feedback that strengthens the conditions of the Loop itself, the growth mechanism may disappear once the external resources are withdrawn.

Amplifying therefore concerns endogenous reinforcement, not growth rate.

# 13. Relationship to Adjacent Theories

The New Loop Theory does not seek to replace existing theories of innovation and strategy. It focuses on a mechanism that lies between several of them: how a New Loop forms and eventually changes the dominant path of value accumulation within a system.

## 13.1 Christensen and Disruptive Innovation

Disruptive Innovation addresses why certain innovations emerging from the margins can eventually reshape a market.

The New Loop Theory is closely related to this question.

Its distinction lies in pushing the explanation further into the causal structure of the commercial system: why can a marginal value-creation mechanism gradually develop its own reinforcing dynamics and cause the advantages on which the established system depends to change?

Disruptive Innovation therefore provides an important problem space, while The New Loop Theory attempts to explain part of the mechanism through system evolution.

## 13.2 First Principles

First Principles helps us move from established assumptions and industry conventions toward the fundamental properties of a system.

The New Loop Theory then asks whether those fundamental properties can be connected into a new mechanism for generating and accumulating value.

First Principles can therefore serve as an important entry point for discovering a New Loop, but the two concepts answer different questions.

## 13.3 Lean Startup

Lean Startup primarily addresses the cost of validation.

If the underlying direction is wrong, an MVP may simply validate the wrong direction more efficiently.

The relationship can therefore be expressed as:

> **The New Loop Theory identifies a Loop worth validating; Lean Startup tests whether that Loop actually works.**

## 13.4 Design Thinking

Design Thinking focuses on discovering what users genuinely need.

The New Loop Theory asks a further question: if that need is addressed, can it create a new value cycle? Can it become part of a new mechanism for generating and accumulating value?

Discovering user value does not automatically create a New Loop.

## 13.5 The Second Curve

The Second Curve reminds organizations not to wait until the existing business declines before searching for another growth trajectory.

The New Loop Theory asks a deeper question: **Why can a second curve emerge in the first place?**

From the New Loop perspective, the emergence of a new curve involves initial momentum, edge conditions, positive feedback, and eventual system migration.

## 13.6 Strategy

Innovation strategy can easily fall back into the logic of direct competition.

The New Loop Theory therefore reframes a strategic question:

> **“Is this a war worth fighting?”**

This has a natural affinity with Roger Martin's idea of “Play in your field and play to win.”

The point is not to enter every battlefield. It is to find a competitive space in which a distinctive Loop can develop its own reinforcing dynamics.

# 14. Scope and Limitations

The New Loop Theory studies how New Loops form and accumulate within commercial systems and how they may eventually change the dominant path of value accumulation. Its research object therefore has clear boundaries.

## 14.1 Not a Theory of Organizational Change

The New Loop Theory does not attempt to explain organizational culture, organizational management, or the full mechanics of organizational innovation.

It can explain why mature systems may resist another Loop and why a New Loop may require room to develop.

It does not, however, provide a complete answer to how an organization should execute transformation.

## 14.2 Not a Predictive Model

The 3A framework describes necessary conditions and dynamic relationships involved in the formation and evolution of a New Loop. It is not intended to predict whether a specific innovation will succeed.

The theory can help identify structures, but it cannot calculate the probability that a particular New Loop will become Ascendant.

## 14.3 It Cannot Eliminate Uncertainty in Complex Systems

Participants in commercial systems continuously learn, adapt, and respond. Technology, policy, capital, social conditions, and competitor behavior can also change system conditions over time.

The New Loop Theory therefore provides a way of observing and explaining system evolution. It does not eliminate uncertainty.

## 14.4 Not a Unified Theory of All Innovation

The New Loop Theory focuses on a specific pattern of innovation: a new mechanism for generating and accumulating value emerges within an existing system and may gradually change the dominant path of value accumulation.

Large amounts of incremental innovation, product optimization, and efficiency improvement within an existing Loop therefore fall outside the core research object of the theory.

## 14.5 A New Loop Is Not Guaranteed to Succeed

A New Loop may possess structural asymmetry and some degree of endogenous reinforcement and still fail to become the dominant path of the system.

It may be constrained by insufficient resources, timing, external conditions, competitive responses, or another Loop that proves stronger.

Discovering a New Loop therefore does not imply that it will inevitably become Ascendant.

The theoretical question is instead to understand under what conditions systemic migration can occur, and under what conditions it does not.

# 15. Research Agenda

V1.0 establishes a relatively complete conceptual foundation: the commercial system, Old Loop, New Loop, Stock, Gain Function, Attractor, and the 3A framework together form the basic theoretical architecture.

The next stage of research should not focus on endlessly adding concepts. It should focus on testing the causal relationships among the concepts that already exist.

First, the theory needs a more rigorous boundary for **Asymmetric**. How much difference in value-generation logic is sufficient to constitute structural asymmetry? How can a genuine New Loop be distinguished from a parameter change within the Old Loop? These questions determine whether “New Loop” can function as a discriminating theoretical concept rather than simply a label for novel outcomes.

Second, the distinction between **Amplifying** and ordinary growth requires further investigation. What kind of feedback qualifies as genuinely endogenous reinforcement? Under what conditions do capital, subsidies, network effects, scale effects, and accumulated organizational capabilities constitute reinforcing mechanisms? These questions require further case analysis and counterexample testing.

Third, and perhaps most importantly, the theory needs a more precise account of how a change in the **Gain Function** actually produces an **Attractor Shift**. Once a New Loop changes system conditions, why and when does the value of existing Stock change? At what point does that change become sufficient to weaken the reinforcing capacity of the Old Loop? How does a local New Loop gradually become the new dominant path of value accumulation?

The theory also needs a broader and more rigorous case base. Case studies should not merely demonstrate that the theory can explain successful examples. They should test its boundaries across different industries, competitive structures, and stages of development, while using counterexamples to identify conditions under which the theory fails.

Ultimately, The New Loop Theory needs to evolve from a framework with explanatory power into a theory that can withstand continuous testing, generate meaningful counterexamples, and clearly specify the boundaries of its own validity.

> **The value of a theory does not lie in how many successful cases it can explain. It lies in whether it can tell us what it should explain, what it should not explain, and under what conditions it might be wrong.**
