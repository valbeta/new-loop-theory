## About This Case Collection

This case collection accompanies **The New Loop Theory (NLT) v1.3.0**. The theory itself asks how a new value-creation loop forms within an existing competitive system, and why certain new loops can gradually alter the direction in which value accumulates. The cases serve a different purpose: they put these concepts back into real business environments and examine what they actually look like when they encounter the complexity of reality.

The cases in this collection are therefore not presented as one-by-one demonstrations of theoretical concepts, nor are they intended to prove that a particular company “fits” or “does not fit” NLT. Real competitive systems do not unfold according to the sequence of a theory's chapters. Asymmetry, Asymmetric Iteration, Core Loop, Core Stock, Amplifying, Stock Migration, and Ascendance may occur simultaneously, interact with one another, or constrain one another. A case may reveal only part of this structure. It is precisely these incomplete, asynchronous, and sometimes conflicting phenomena that make cases valuable for theoretical inquiry.

The cases are therefore better understood as a set of **observational samples**. They are brought together not to construct a path from failure to success, but to expose different theoretical mechanisms to pressure across different competitive systems.

When examining a case, the first step is to return to the competitive system in which it operates. How was value originally created, acquired, and accumulated? Why did users make choices in the way they did? Why did firms organize resources in the way they did? Which stocks therefore retained value over time? When a new participant entered the system, which relationship actually changed?

Only then do further questions become meaningful. Did the new value relationship form a different loop? Did existing stocks acquire new roles? Did any stock begin to reinforce the system's ability to create value? As system conditions continued to change, did previously advantaged stocks retain the same gain function? If not, where did the dominant direction of value accumulation migrate?

These questions are not intended to form a fixed analytical procedure. They are simply several coordinates to which we can repeatedly return when observing a competitive system.

### About the Case Material

This case collection is based on publicly available business histories and industry materials. Its purpose is theoretical observation rather than rigorous business historiography or empirical validation. Some figures, time periods, and descriptions of companies are used to establish the context of observation; the theoretical interpretations are analyses made through the conceptual framework of NLT.

It is especially important to note that the analyses presented here are not intended as the only possible interpretations. Existing case analyses included in this collection are themselves observations made by particular observers through particular theoretical lenses. As the theory develops, the same case may yield different observations.

That is also why this companion case collection exists: **theory should not be used to end the discussion around a case, and cases should not be used to prove that a theory is already correct. The two should remain in continuous dialogue.**

# 1. Quibi: When a New Way of Watching Could Not Find a New Value-Creation Structure

**Observation period: 2018–2020**

Quibi's story can easily be summarized as a failed streaming startup: a group of Hollywood veterans raised substantial capital, invested in high-cost content, and attempted to build a new video platform around fragmented moments of mobile viewing, only to announce its shutdown roughly six months after launch.

The story is harder to explain if we look only at product or market demand. Mobile viewing was clearly a real behavior, and short-form video had already demonstrated the commercial value of fragmented attention. What makes Quibi worth examining is therefore not simply why users did not embrace it, but what it actually changed—and what it left unchanged.

### From Mobile Viewing to a New Value Proposition

Quibi's original proposition contained a fairly clear asymmetry. Traditional filmed entertainment was generally organized around longer viewing sessions, home screens, or relatively fixed viewing contexts. Quibi attempted to compress professionally produced, high-quality content into shorter segments better suited to mobile contexts, while designing the viewing experience specifically around the smartphone.

From the perspective of how users acquired value, this did change one dimension. Users no longer needed to set aside an uninterrupted block of time to watch professionally produced content; they could consume it during commutes, while waiting, or in other fragmented moments.

The problem was that this change occurred primarily on the demand side.

Quibi did not create a comparably different structure for producing content itself. High production costs, professional production teams, star talent, and the established economics of the entertainment industry remained in place. The content became shorter, but that did not make it materially easier to produce. The viewing context changed, while the cost structure of creating the content did not enter a corresponding new structure.

Once the element of the “new experience” is set aside, the underlying value relationship on both the demand and supply sides still rested on the familiar structure of content production, rights acquisition, and distribution:

**Content investment → Content production → Distribution through subscription platform → Revenue return → Next round of content investment**

This created an interesting tension: Quibi attempted to support a new consumption relationship with an old content-production structure.

This does not mean that short-form content had no value, nor that professionally produced content could not adapt to mobile devices. The important point is that a change on the demand side did not automatically produce a structural change on the supply side. The new way of watching still needed to find a new value coupling with the existing way of creating content, and Quibi did not appear to establish such a relationship.

### A Structure That Was Never Fully Tested

Seen from this perspective, Quibi's rapid failure was not simply a matter of product-market fit.

Before launch, Quibi committed substantial capital and pre-purchased and produced large volumes of content. This allowed the platform to launch with a significant content offering, but it also reduced the room available to reconfigure its value relationships in response to actual user feedback. What the platform needed to establish was not simply whether users were willing to watch, but whether users, content production, distribution, and the business model could form a new relationship capable of sustained reinforcement.

This is why [Asymmetric Iteration](placeholder) is particularly relevant to the case.

A new value structure is rarely designed completely in advance. What matters is not simply how compelling the initial asymmetry appears, but whether the innovator can continuously adjust the value relationship once real-world feedback arrives. Quibi's large capital base may have made this exploration more rigid: it had ample resources to execute its original assumptions, but not necessarily an equally strong structure for continuously revising those assumptions.

From this perspective, Quibi is more than a story of “demand-side asymmetry failing.” It raises a broader question: **Asymmetry is only a structural hypothesis; the real challenge is allowing that hypothesis to find, through real-world feedback, a value relationship capable of self-reinforcement.**

### What the Case Actually Exposes

If we observe Quibi through NLT, the most useful takeaway is not the conclusion that “it failed to create supply-side asymmetry.” That is only one possible interpretation. The more important question it raises is this:

When an innovation first emerges from one side of the user-value relationship, does the other side contain a new way of creating value that can couple with it?

If it does not, the new demand dimension may remain dependent on the old loop. It may create a new experience and even generate scale in the short term through capital, marketing, or content investment, while still failing to form a new value-accumulation structure.

Quibi therefore offers a useful counter-observation: **a genuinely new way of acquiring value can exist without being sufficient to form a New Loop.**

# 2. WeWork: New Value in Space, Old Ways of Realizing That Value

**Observation period: 2010–2019**

WeWork is more complicated than Quibi because it did change the way office space was used and evaluated.

The basic relationship in traditional commercial real estate is relatively straightforward: companies or individuals need workspace, while property owners generate rental income by holding or leasing real estate. Property, capital, and long-term leases therefore form important stock structures within the system.

> **Acquire property → Hold or lease long-term → Rent out → Rental income → Expand property base**

WeWork reframed office space as something closer to a service. Flexible terms, standardized design, community activities, shared facilities, and services for startups and corporate customers meant that office space was no longer simply a measure of square footage. For many users, what they were buying was not merely a collection of desks, but a more flexible working relationship.

From the user side, the change was real.

### What Did “Space as a Service” Actually Change?

If we unpack the logic behind user choice, traditional office space was generally evaluated through dimensions such as location, floor area, rent, and property conditions. WeWork added flexibility, service experience, workplace environment, and community relationships as new dimensions of value.

WeWork therefore did contain an element of asymmetry.

But the question immediately shifts to the other side: how were these new forms of user value actually created?

WeWork's core operating model still depended heavily on acquiring property, fitting it out, and then renting it to users on shorter and more flexible terms. It reorganized a relatively coarse-grained real-estate product into a more granular, service-intensive one, but it did not fundamentally escape the term structure, capital requirements, and operating costs of real estate itself.

The more useful question is therefore not whether “Space as a Service” was real, but something more specific: **Did the new dimensions of user value also change the way value was created on the supply side?**

If not, the new value still had to depend on the existing supply structure.

### Why Scale Did Not Automatically Become Structural Capability

During its expansion, WeWork accumulated large numbers of users, locations, brand recognition, and community relationships. All of these can be considered stocks, but the **[Core Stock](placeholder)** in NLT is not simply a general term for valuable resources owned by a company.

The key question is whether these stocks carry a structure capable of continuously strengthening the new value-creation capability.

More locations, for example, can provide more choices; more users can create a larger community network; a stronger brand can improve customer acquisition. But if continued growth still requires continuously adding more properties, fit-outs, and operating costs, growth itself does not demonstrate the existence of a Core Loop capable of self-reinforcement.

This is why WeWork is a useful case for maintaining distance from the language of the “growth flywheel.”

A system can contain reinforcing growth relationships while simultaneously containing stronger balancing forces. More members can support further expansion of space, but expanding space also creates more long-term lease obligations and operating costs. Capital can temporarily connect these processes, but capital itself does not automatically create endogenous Amplifying.

The failed 2019 IPO attempt and the subsequent reset in valuation made this structure more visible. From a theoretical perspective, however, the more important question is not simply that the company struggled once capital support weakened. It is whether the stocks accumulated during the expansion could continue to strengthen value creation once external momentum was reduced.

If they could not, the preceding growth was more likely an expansion process driven substantially by external resources than a New Loop with an independent internal dynamic.

### The Theoretical Question WeWork Leaves Behind

WeWork is valuable to the theory because it forces us to distinguish between two things that are easy to conflate.

First, whether a new form of user value genuinely exists. Second, whether that new user value has already become a new value-creation structure.

The two are not equivalent.

WeWork therefore should not simply be reduced to “the demand side changed while the supply side did not.” A more useful observation is this: **Once a new form of user value has been created, does it change the role of stocks within the system, and can it establish a new reinforcing relationship?**

This also gives WeWork and Quibi something in common. Both show that a new dimension of value does not by itself guarantee the formation of a New Loop. But their problems are not identical. Quibi more clearly exposes a break between the value hypothesis and real-world feedback, while WeWork more clearly illustrates the tension created when new user value coexists with an old supply structure over an extended period.

# 3. Shared Bikes: Why Rapid Growth Can Still Fail to Become Amplifying

**Observation period: 2015–2019**

Shared bikes are particularly useful for examining **[Amplifying](placeholder)** because the market displayed extraordinarily rapid growth over a short period.

As the number of bikes increased, users could find bikes more easily. As the user base grew, more riding data accumulated. Greater coverage then attracted more users. The early shared-bike market therefore appeared to contain a very typical reinforcing relationship:

**More bikes → Greater availability → More users → More rides → More bikes deployed**

Viewed only through the growth curve, this looks almost exactly like a growth flywheel. But growth itself does not mean that a loop is Amplifying.

### Another Set of Forces Exists Beneath the Growth Loop

The difficulty with shared bikes is that the bikes themselves are not digital assets that become easier to maintain as scale increases.

Bikes wear out. They require repair, replacement, repositioning, and maintenance. As the fleet expands, the physical operating area expands as well, increasing the complexity of maintenance and dispatch. At the same time as user growth, another set of balancing relationships therefore emerges:

**More bikes → More wear and tear → More repair and replacement**

**More bikes → Larger dispatching area → Greater operational complexity**

Both dynamics exist simultaneously.

Shared-bike growth therefore cannot, by itself, demonstrate the presence of **[Net Amplifying](placeholder)**. The real question is whether, as the system expands, the reinforcing relationships increasingly improve the system's own capacity to accumulate value, or whether every round of growth simultaneously creates greater costs and resistance.

That distinction matters more than whether a system appears to have a “flywheel.”

### Why Data Did Not Automatically Become Core Stock

Shared-bike companies did accumulate users, orders, riding trajectories, and dispatching data. It is easy to call these things “core assets,” but doing so can obscure the question the theory actually needs to ask.

**Can the data enter a new value-creation structure?**

If the data only helps a company determine more accurately where bikes are or when people ride, without fundamentally changing the cost relationships created by depreciation, physical maintenance, and dispatching, then the value of the data remains primarily one of local optimization.

**It may improve efficiency without necessarily becoming Core Stock.**

This distinction also explains why having “a lot of data” does not directly imply the formation of Core Stock. The defining characteristic of Core Stock is not scarcity or quantity, but whether a stock carries the core value-creation capability of the New Loop and can, in turn, reinforce that capability.

The user data accumulated by shared-bike companies did not clearly complete this structural transformation.

### What Does “Pseudo-Amplifying” Mean?

Shared bikes are therefore particularly useful for examining the boundary of Amplifying.

“Pseudo-Amplifying” does not mean that the system contains no reinforcing relationships at all. It means that the visible reinforcing relationships are insufficient to overcome the increasingly strong balancing forces within the system. External capital can push the system to expand rapidly, but it cannot thereby change the underlying structure.

This is why the shared-bike case matters to NLT: it reminds us that **Amplifying describes a causal structure, not a growth outcome.**

A company can grow extremely quickly and still fail to form a loop capable of continuously accumulating its own value-creation capability.

# 4. BlackBerry: When the Stock Remains, Why Does Ascendance Still Migrate?

**Observation period: 1999–2013**

BlackBerry is particularly useful for examining [Ascendance](placeholder) and [Sequential Ascendance](placeholder), because traditional business histories can easily reduce the story to a simple narrative: BlackBerry led the market, failed to keep up after the iPhone appeared, and was eventually displaced.

That account is not entirely wrong, but it does not reach the question that matters most for NLT.

What BlackBerry actually lost was not all of its value, users, or capabilities. What it lost was **the ability of its existing stocks to continue carrying the dominant path of value accumulation under new system conditions.**

### What Had BlackBerry Accumulated?

BlackBerry's advantage was built around an enterprise mobile communications system. The device, secure communications, BlackBerry Enterprise Server, enterprise IT management, and enterprise procurement relationships formed a structure that could not easily be reduced to any one component.

BlackBerry's Core Stock therefore cannot be reduced to “phone sales” or “brand.”

A more theoretically meaningful interpretation is a Structural Stock: an installed enterprise base, security and compliance capabilities, IT relationships, and the organizational inertia built around this system together formed the structure through which BlackBerry's value was accumulated.

> **Enterprise mobile communication demand → Secure communication capability → Enterprise deployment → Enterprise IT relationships and usage inertia → More deployment**

Under the system conditions of the time, this structure was extremely strong. The deeper the enterprise deployment, the higher the migration cost. The more widespread the organizational use, the more processes and management practices became organized around it. The existing stock therefore had a powerful gain function.

BlackBerry did not become dominant simply because it had a particularly good phone. It became dominant because the structure it accumulated was worth continuing to accumulate under the competitive conditions of the time.

### What Happened to the Stock When the System Changed?

The emergence of smartphones changed the system conditions.

Mobile devices increasingly evolved from communication terminals into general-purpose computing platforms. Application ecosystems, developer participation, user scale, and third-party software began to become new directions of value accumulation.

> **User scale → Developer participation → Application ecosystem → User value → More users**

The most important observation at this point is that BlackBerry's original stocks did not suddenly become zero.

Enterprise security capabilities still had value. Enterprise customer relationships still existed. The brand still existed. What changed was the **[gain function](placeholder)** of those stocks.

The same security capabilities no longer determined the primary direction of value accumulation within a system increasingly driven by general-purpose computing platforms and application ecosystems. At the same time, developer ecosystems, application breadth, and user scale began to acquire stronger system-level gains.

What happened, then, was not simply a “BlackBerry versus iPhone” competitive loss. The dominant path of value accumulation had migrated.

### Ascendance and Sequential Ascendance

This is why [Ascendant within the 3A framework](placeholder) is more usefully understood as a system state than as a ranking among firms.

A New Loop becoming dominant does not mean that it will retain Ascendance permanently. System conditions continue to change, and what the system rewards continued accumulation of changes with them.

Sequential Ascendance therefore describes the continuing migration of the dominant path of value accumulation.

The BlackBerry case makes one point particularly clear: **a stock can remain in existence, and its value can remain real, while Ascendance has already migrated elsewhere.**

That is closer to the system change NLT seeks to describe than simply saying that a company “failed.”

# 5. Skype: Network Effects Also Enter New System Conditions

**Observation period: 2003–2013**

Skype's early history is almost a classic example of network effects.

Traditional international communications were built around carrier networks and distance-based costs.

> Communication infrastructure → Time/distance-based billing → Communication revenue → Network investment

Skype reorganized communication relationships through the Internet, making cross-border communication less dependent on the traditional economics of telecommunications. As the number of users increased, the network itself became more valuable; as the network became more valuable, it attracted more users.

This created a clear reinforcing relationship:

**More users → More people to contact → Greater communication value → More users**

The user network therefore became an important stock for Skype.

### Why Could the Network Itself Become Value?

Skype's significance was not simply that it made traditional telephone calls cheaper. It changed the way communication value was accumulated.

In traditional telecommunications, network infrastructure was the primary carrier of value. Skype instead made the user network itself an important carrier of value. Every additional user could increase the number of people other users could reach.

In this sense, Skype did form a new value-accumulation relationship, and for a period it became an important dominant path for Internet-based communication.

### But New System Conditions Continue to Emerge

Over time, however, the central problems in communications began to change.

Enterprise users increasingly needed more than the ability to contact another person. Stable video conferencing, multi-party collaboration, organizational management, meeting controls, and integration with enterprise workflows became more important.

A new value-accumulation relationship therefore began to form around a different set of capabilities.

An interesting phenomenon followed: Skype's original network effect did not suddenly disappear. Users remained. Contacts remained. The network still had value.

What changed was this: **the user network could no longer, by itself, define the most important direction of value accumulation within the system.**

### Migration of Ascendance, Not the Disappearance of the Old Loop

Skype therefore shares an important characteristic with BlackBerry.

Neither exited a position of Ascendance because its original stock suddenly lost all value. What changed was the system conditions and, with them, the gain function of those stocks.

This was true of BlackBerry's enterprise security system and of Skype's user network.

This makes **[Sequential Ascendance](placeholder)** more than a concept describing “who replaced whom.” It becomes a way of describing system evolution: **the system continually redefines what is worth accumulating and which feedback relationships can continue to generate value.**

# 6. Xiaomi: Stocks Are Not Simply Moved; Their Roles Change Within a New Loop

**Observation period: 2010–2016**

Xiaomi's early development is particularly useful for examining the relationship among Core Loop, Core Stock, [Asymmetric Iteration](placeholder), and [Stock Migration](placeholder), because Xiaomi did not begin with a fully formed business system.

Around 2010, Android already existed, the smartphone industry was already established, and user feedback, online communities, and software iteration were hardly Xiaomi's inventions. What is worth observing is how Xiaomi connected these existing elements in a different way.

### MIUI: From Users as an Endpoint to Users as Part of the Loop

The value relationship initially established around MIUI can be summarized as:

**User participation → Feedback → Product iteration → Improved user experience → Deeper user participation**

The important point is not “user feedback” itself. Almost any software product can collect feedback. What was distinctive about Xiaomi's early approach was the degree to which user participation was embedded in the product iteration process, with frequent updates providing a continuing response to that participation.

This changed the relationship between users and the product.

Users were no longer simply the endpoint after product delivery. They became part of the product's continuing evolution. Each act of participation could generate new feedback, and each piece of feedback could enter the next round of product iteration.

This is where the Core Loop becomes worth observing.

### Asymmetric Iteration Is Not an Add-On Activity

Xiaomi's early iteration rhythm also shows that an asymmetric structure does not necessarily exist in complete form from the beginning.

Weekly updates, user feedback, and product adjustments continuously reconfigured the value relationship in real-world conditions. Some features were strengthened, others abandoned, and user needs continued to evolve.

Asymmetric Iteration is therefore better understood as an ongoing exploration of structure. The innovator is not mechanically executing within a fully determined New Loop, but continuously deciding, through real-world feedback, which value relationships deserve further reinforcement and which connections need to be reconfigured.

This also means that Core Loop and Asymmetric Iteration should not be treated as a strict sequence.

The loop is being strengthened while it is simultaneously being revised.

### What Became Core Stock?

If Xiaomi's early advantage is measured only by the number of users, it is easy to miss the stock that mattered more.

What is more interesting is the user relationship itself: high engagement, trust, product identification, and the mechanisms through which users could continue to participate and provide feedback.

These are not simple numbers, nor can they be fully represented by a single KPI. They are closer to a Structural Stock—a combination of interrelated states that together form a structure capable of carrying value-creation capability.

> **User relationships + User trust + Participation mechanisms + Feedback mechanisms + Product iteration capability**

This is also what distinguishes Core Stock from an ordinary Key Stock.

User count can be a Key Stock. But only when user relationships, participation mechanisms, feedback mechanisms, and product iteration capabilities form a stable connection capable of continuously carrying new value-creation capability does the structure more closely resemble what NLT calls Core Stock.

### Where Does Stock Migration Actually Occur?

Xiaomi's later development makes this structure even more interesting.

The user relationships formed during the MIUI period subsequently entered the smartphone hardware business. At that point, the same user relationships no longer served only as a mechanism for “giving product feedback”; they also began to support demand validation, purchase conversion, and word-of-mouth propagation.

> **User participation relationship → Product identification → Early purchase → Word of mouth → New users**

Later, these user relationships entered the IoT and ecosystem businesses. The relationship between users and the brand became a foundation for connecting different devices, and a relationship structure originally serving a single product entered a larger ecosystem.

> **Single-product relationship → Multi-device relationship → Value of device connectivity → Ecosystem synergy**

This is where Stock Migration becomes especially important.

Migration does not mean moving a group of users from the MIUI organization into the smartphone organization and then from the smartphone organization into IoT. What actually migrates is **the role played by the same stock within different value-creation structures**.

A user relationship can perform different functions in different loops and therefore acquire different gain functions.

Stock Migration is therefore not fundamentally a resource-allocation problem. It is a structural problem.

### The Deeper Question Left by Xiaomi

The Xiaomi case also reminds us that the “new” in a New Loop does not mean that all of its constituent elements must be novel.

Users, Android, smartphones, online communities, and hardware supply chains were not inventions unique to Xiaomi. What changed was the relationship among them.

A new value-creation loop therefore does not necessarily require entirely new resources. It may emerge by reorganizing existing stocks so that they enter feedback relationships that did not previously exist.

From this perspective, the relationship between Stock Migration and a New Loop is more complex than the simple idea that “old resources support a new business”: **a stock can take on new value only when it enters a new value-creation relationship.**

# 7. Looking at the Six Cases Together

The easiest mistake to make when looking at these cases together is to search for a single path to success.

Quibi, WeWork, shared bikes, BlackBerry, Skype, and Xiaomi clearly did not develop along the same path. They should not even be reduced to a simple division between “failed cases” and “successful cases.”

What they provide are different angles of observation.

Quibi shows that a new demand-side value proposition can be real and yet fail to find a supply-side value-creation structure capable of supporting it. WeWork demonstrates a related but different situation: user experience and value evaluation can genuinely change while remaining dependent on an existing supply structure, without automatically producing a new value-accumulation logic.

Shared bikes move the question toward Amplifying. The system contained obvious reinforcing growth relationships, but it also faced substantial losses, maintenance requirements, and dispatching complexity. This creates a clear distinction between “growth” and “Amplifying”: the former describes what happened to the system, while the latter requires an explanation of why the system could continue producing that outcome.

BlackBerry and Skype extend the observation across a longer time horizon. Both developed clearly dominant value-accumulation paths and accumulated real and valuable stocks. But as system conditions changed, the gain functions of those stocks changed as well, and the dominant paths of value accumulation continued to migrate. The old loops did not disappear immediately, and the old stocks did not become worthless overnight, but Ascendance had already moved in a new direction.

Xiaomi demonstrates another form of movement: a stock does not necessarily migrate because an old business has ended. Its role can also change continuously as a New Loop forms. User relationships moved from a product-feedback mechanism into hardware sales and then into an ecosystem. The stock was not simply replicated; it acquired new meaning within new value-creation structures.

Taken together, these cases make the boundaries among several NLT concepts clearer.

**Asymmetric** concerns whether the underlying value-creation relationship has changed structurally. Quibi reminds us, however, that novelty on only one side may be insufficient to establish a new value coupling.

**Asymmetric Iteration** concerns how such a structure is continuously adjusted through real-world feedback. Xiaomi shows that this can happen alongside the formation of a Core Loop rather than occupying some fixed “preceding stage.”

**Core Loop** concerns whether a value-creation relationship begins to reinforce itself continuously. Shared bikes remind us that visible growth feedback does not necessarily mean that a Core Loop capable of accumulating value-creation capability has formed.

**Core Stock** is not a generalized term for “the company's most important assets.” It concerns which stocks actually carry the core value-creation capability. BlackBerry's enterprise deployment structure and Xiaomi's user relationships both show that Core Stock can take the form of a Structural Stock rather than a single measurable resource.

**Amplifying** concerns whether such a structure has endogenous reinforcing capacity, rather than whether growth is occurring. The shared-bike case illustrates this distinction particularly clearly.

**Stock Migration** extends the observation from internal resource allocation toward the system level. The value of a stock is not fixed; it depends on the value-creation structure in which it operates and the conditions of the system at a given time. Xiaomi illustrates the migration of a stock's role, while BlackBerry and Skype illustrate how changes in a stock's gain function can accompany the migration of the dominant value-accumulation path.

Finally, **Ascendance** does not describe a state in which a company wins permanently. It describes a value-accumulation path gaining increasing systemic attraction under particular system conditions and gradually becoming dominant. **Sequential Ascendance** further reminds us that this dominance itself remains subject to system evolution.

The six cases therefore cannot be arranged into a fixed sequence of “Asymmetric → Core Loop → Core Stock → Amplifying → Ascendance.”

A New Loop in reality is closer to a continually changing structure.

A new value relationship may emerge first and then change through iteration. A Core Loop may begin forming before the structure has fully stabilized. Certain stocks may only gradually reveal their role as Core Stock after a new loop has begun to form. Amplifying and Asymmetric Iteration may occur simultaneously. Stock Migration may recur at different stages. Whether a new dominant path of value accumulation ultimately emerges depends on how the competitive system continues to evolve.

## 8. Cases as Reflexive Observation of Theory

Perhaps the most important role of a companion case collection lies precisely here.

If cases exist only to illustrate a theory, it becomes very easy to compress reality into what the theory already knows: find a company, identify its Asymmetry, Core Loop, and Core Stock, and then use those concepts to explain why it succeeded or failed.

That approach can be useful for teaching, but it is not sufficient for theoretical inquiry.

A genuinely useful case observation should allow reality to question the theory.

Quibi forces us to ask: under what conditions can demand-side asymmetry cross over into the supply side?

WeWork forces us to ask: once user value changes, when can we say that the gain function on the supply side has genuinely changed?

Shared bikes force us to distinguish between the existence of reinforcing relationships and the existence of sufficient Net Amplifying potential within the system.

BlackBerry and Skype force us to reconsider what happens when a stock continues to exist and create value, but no longer carries system-level Ascendance.

Xiaomi leaves us with a further question: what exactly is the relationship between the formation of Core Stock and Stock Migration? When a Structural Stock enters a new loop, does it retain the same structure, or does the stock itself undergo restructuring through the migration process?

These questions do not all need definitive answers in the current version of the theory.

In fact, if every case in a case collection fits perfectly into the theoretical framework, that should itself be cause for concern. It may mean that the cases have been shaped in advance by the theory rather than that the theory has genuinely confronted reality.

NLT therefore treats case observation as a form of **Reflexive Observation**: the theory provides a way of observing, cases expose structures in reality, and those structures in turn force the theory to re-examine its own boundaries.

From this perspective, case observation is never merely decorative proof of a theory.

**Its real value is to keep the theory incomplete in the face of reality.**

## Version Status of the Case Collection

This case collection remains relatively independent from the main theory text. The theory defines concepts, proposes mechanisms, and establishes theoretical boundaries; the case collection places those concepts back into specific competitive systems.

The judgments presented in the case collection should therefore not be understood as a final application template for the theory.

As The New Loop Theory continues to develop, the same cases may be re-observed through later versions of the theory, while new cases may reveal phenomena that the current theory does not yet adequately explain. The case collection is therefore not a closed “case answer library,” but part of the ongoing theoretical inquiry.

**Theory → Observation → Mechanism → Re-observation**

This cycle itself is part of the research practice that NLT needs to maintain over time.
